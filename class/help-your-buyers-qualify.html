What we'll cover

Six ways a buyer can qualify for more than the first lender said

Every one of these is a real program with real rules. We'll go through who each one fits, what it takes to document, and — just as important — when it doesn't work. The specific lenders and their current guidelines are named live on the class.

One

Stated income for the self-employed

Insured programs that let a business owner qualify on what the business actually earns, instead of the write-down on their Notice of Assessment. Two years in business is usually the gate.

Two

The basement suite that doesn't exist yet

Purchase Plus Improvements with a lender that will consider projected rent from a suite being built after closing. The reno money comes in at purchase — and the future rent can help carry the mortgage.

Three

Bank-statement income

Alternative lenders who read twelve months of deposits and a declaration of income instead of tax returns. This also covers buyers with no registered business and gig or rideshare income.

Four

Money sitting in the corporation

For incorporated buyers: a bank program that uses the operating company's after-tax net income to bring the debt-service ratio down. Strict ownership and shareholder rules — we'll cover exactly what they are.

Five

Grossing up business income

How a lender can gross up business, professional or commission income — how far they'll go, what credit score it costs, and the one rule that trips people up: gross-up or add-backs, never both.

Six

Debts the business pays

The vehicle loan running through the business account: when it counts against your buyer as a personal liability and when it's treated as a business expense. Getting this wrong quietly kills approvals.

➕ Plus, the two files everyone assumes are dead: a past bankruptcy or consumer proposal — some lenders will look, some absolutely will not — and the buyer whose deal fell apart on financing last year.
