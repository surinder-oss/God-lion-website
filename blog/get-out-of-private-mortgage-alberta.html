Getting Out of a Private Mortgage in Alberta: A Step-by-Step Exit Plan

Book a Free Consultation

Pick a time that works for you — no obligation

The private mortgage did its job. It got you the house, or kept you in it, when the bank wouldn't. Now the one-year term is running out, the renewal fee is coming, and you're wondering how long this has to last.

You can get out of a private mortgage. Most people who plan for it do. The ones who stay stuck usually didn't start early enough, or didn't know what the next lender would need. This guide walks through the exit, step by step, for homeowners in Calgary and across Alberta.

Why a Private Mortgage Is Meant to Be Short

A private mortgage is a bridge, not a home. Private lenders lend mostly on the equity in your property. They care less about your credit score or how your income looks on paper. That's why they can say yes when a bank says no.

The trade-off is cost. Private mortgages usually come with a higher interest rate than a bank or a B-lender, plus a lender fee and often a broker fee taken at the start. Terms are short, often 6 to 24 months, and one year is common. Many are interest-only, so your balance doesn't go down while you pay.

That's fine for a year. It gets expensive over three or four. A CMHC research brief on exit strategies looked at borrowers with alternative lenders and flagged the main risk: people who never improve their file end up stuck in high-cost mortgages for years. The exit is the whole point. If you want the basics on how these loans work first, our guide to private mortgage lenders in Calgary covers cost and when they make sense.

Step One: Read Your Commitment Before Anything Else

Before you plan the exit, find your mortgage commitment and read four things. They decide how much the exit costs and how much time you have.

  • The maturity date. This is your deadline. Write it down and count back six months.
  • The prepayment terms. Some private mortgages are fully open, so you can pay them off any time. Others are closed for the first few months, or charge a few months' interest if you leave early.
  • The renewal terms. Look for a renewal fee and any automatic renewal clause. Some contracts renew on their own, fee included, if nobody acts in time.
  • The discharge process. Who prepares the payout statement, what they charge, and how much notice they want. Our post on what it costs to discharge a mortgage in Alberta explains the usual fees.

If anything in the commitment is unclear, ask your lawyer or your broker to walk you through it. Don't guess on a contract like this.

How to Get Out of a Private Mortgage: The Four Exit Paths

There are four real ways out. Which one fits depends on your credit, your income, and how much equity you have today. Here's how each one works.

1. Refinance with a B-lender

For most people, this is the next step down in cost. B-lenders (also called alternative lenders) sit between the banks and private money. They look at the whole file, not just the score.

  • Some B-lenders can work with credit scores below 600, depending on the rest of the file.
  • They can often allow debt ratios up to roughly 50% for both GDS and TDS, higher than the usual bank limits.
  • For self-employed borrowers, many can use about 12 months of business bank statements to show income, without the most recent Notice of Assessment.

B-lender mortgages usually have one- to three-year terms and still cost more than a bank. Treat them as the middle step. Our guide to B-lender mortgages in Calgary goes deeper on who qualifies.

2. Refinance straight back to a bank or credit union

This is usually where your costs come down the most, and it's possible if your file has recovered. A-lenders generally want steady, provable income, credit that has been rebuilt with a clean recent history, and debt ratios inside their limits. For a refinance, most cap the new mortgage at 80% of your home's value.

If you're self-employed, A-lenders often use a two-year average of your declared income, or a stated-income program for business owners. If you came out of a bankruptcy or consumer proposal, many banks want to see a couple of years of re-established credit after it's done. Some files can jump straight from private to an A-lender. Many go private to B to A.

3. Renew the private mortgage, with a plan

Sometimes you're just not ready. Maybe the credit needs six more months, or your business needs another tax year on file. Renewing for one more term can be the right call, as long as it comes with a written plan for what changes before the next maturity. Ask about the renewal fee and whether the lender will lower anything now that you've paid on time.

4. Sell the property

If none of the lending options work and the costs keep climbing, selling can protect the equity you've built. It's nobody's first choice. But selling on your own timeline usually leaves you with more than waiting until the lender forces the issue.

A Timeline to Get Out of a Private Mortgage on Time

The single biggest mistake we see is starting too late. Lenders need time, and so does your credit. Here's a simple timeline that works backwards from your maturity date.

  1. The day the private mortgage funds. Set up every payment on auto-pay. Write down the exit target: which kind of lender, and what has to change to get there.
  2. Every month after. Pay every bill on time. Keep at least two credit accounts open and in use, like a credit card and a small loan or secured card. Keep card balances low, ideally under about 30% of the limit.
  3. Six months before maturity. Pull your credit reports from Equifax and TransUnion (both are free) and fix any errors. Gather your income documents. Get a sense of what your home is worth now.
  4. Three to four months before maturity. Apply. Most lenders will look at a refinance in this window, and it leaves time for an appraisal, conditions and a backup plan.
  5. Thirty days before maturity. Your new mortgage should be approved and the lawyers instructed. If it isn't, talk to your private lender now about a short extension, and get it in writing.

What the Next Lender Will Look For

A B-lender and a bank will ask the same basic questions. You can work on every one of them while you're still in the private mortgage.

  • Payment history. On-time payments on the private mortgage and everything else. A late payment in the last year is hard to explain.
  • Equity. Private lenders usually need you to keep 20% to 25% or more of the home's value as equity. Your next lender will order its own appraisal. If values have dropped, there's less room to refinance.
  • Income. Pay stubs and a letter if you're employed. Bank statements, Notices of Assessment or financial statements if you're self-employed. Get these ready early.
  • The story. What happened, and what's different now. A short, honest letter explaining the credit problem and how it was fixed helps more than people think.
  • Other debts. Collections, tax arrears, or a private second mortgage all need a plan. Sometimes the refinance pays them out at the same time.

If you also have a private second mortgage behind a bank first mortgage, the exit often means rolling both into one new mortgage. That only works if there's enough equity, so the numbers need to be checked early.

What If You Can't Get Out of a Private Mortgage in Time?

Don't let the mortgage mature without a deal in place. Once the term ends unpaid, you're in default. That can bring extra fees and interest, and in Alberta the lender can start a court process to collect. Our guide to how foreclosure works in Alberta explains what that looks like.

Instead, call your private lender before the maturity date. Ask what a renewal or short extension costs, and get the terms in writing. Then use that time to finish whatever is holding up the exit. Private lenders would usually rather renew a borrower who pays on time than chase one who doesn't.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long should you stay in a private mortgage?

As short as you can. Most private mortgages are written for about one year, and the plan should be to move to a B-lender or a bank by the first or second maturity. Staying longer means paying more fees and interest each year.

Can I pay off a private mortgage early?

It depends on your contract. Some private mortgages are fully open, so you can pay them off any time without a penalty. Others are closed for the first few months or charge a few months' interest. Check the prepayment section of your commitment.

Can I go from a private mortgage straight to a bank?

Sometimes. If your credit has been rebuilt, your income is easy to prove and you have enough equity, a bank or credit union may refinance you directly. Many people move to a B-lender first and then to a bank at the next renewal.

What happens if my private mortgage matures and I can't refinance?

Talk to your lender before the maturity date. Many will offer a renewal or short extension for a fee. If the mortgage matures unpaid, you're in default, and the lender can start a legal process to collect. Selling on your own timeline is another option.

When should I start planning my exit from a private mortgage?

The day it funds. Start working on your credit right away, gather documents about six months before maturity, and apply for the new mortgage three to four months before the term ends.
