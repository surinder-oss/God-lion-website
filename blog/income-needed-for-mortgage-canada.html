How Much Income Do You Need for a $200K, $300K, $400K or $500K Mortgage?

You found a home you like, and the first question in your head is simple. Can I afford this? Most people type it straight into Google as a dollar figure: how much do you have to earn to qualify for a $200,000 mortgage, or a $500,000 one?

So how much income do you need for a mortgage? It depends on the size of the loan, your other debts, and the rate a lender has to test you at. This guide gives you a plain table by mortgage size, shows how debts and amortization move the number, and explains what changes if a bank says your income falls short.

The Two Ratios Lenders Use to Size Your Mortgage

Lenders don't guess how much you can borrow. They use two simple ratios, and your income has to be high enough to pass both.

  • Gross Debt Service (GDS): your mortgage payment, property tax and heating, plus half of any condo fees, as a share of your gross monthly income. For an insured mortgage, the limit is 39%.
  • Total Debt Service (TDS): everything in GDS, plus your other monthly payments. That means car loans, student loans, credit card minimums and lines of credit. The limit is 44%.

Those limits come straight from CMHC's guide to calculating GDS and TDS. Most banks and credit unions use the same limits on uninsured files too, give or take a little.

There's one more piece. Lenders don't test you at the rate you'll actually pay. They test you at the qualifying rate, which is the higher of your contract rate plus 2%, or 5.25%. This is the stress test. It's why the income you need is higher than a simple payment calculator suggests.

Flip It Around: How Much Mortgage Can You Get on Your Salary?

A lot of people search the other way. "How much mortgage can I get with a $70,000 salary?" Here are rough answers using the same assumptions, tested at 6.5% and at 5.25%.

  • $50,000 a year: roughly $200,000 to $230,000
  • $70,000 a year: roughly $290,000 to $330,000
  • $100,000 a year: roughly $430,000 to $470,000
  • $150,000 a year: roughly $650,000 to $720,000

This is household income. If two people are on the mortgage, lenders add both incomes together. A couple earning $70,000 and $50,000 has $120,000 to work with, which supports a mortgage of roughly $510,000 when tested at 6.5% with no other debts.

Keep in mind this is the most a lender may allow, not what you should borrow. Your real budget should leave room for savings, childcare, repairs and life.

How Your Debts Change the Income You Need

The table above assumes you owe nothing else. Most people do. Once you add other payments, the TDS limit of 44% often becomes the one that stops the file.

Here's a simple example. Say you earn $100,000 a year and you're tested at 6.5%:

  • No other debts: you may qualify for about $430,000.
  • A $600 car payment: that drops to about $400,000.
  • $1,000 a month in car and card payments: it drops to about $350,000.

That's $80,000 of buying power gone because of monthly payments. Lenders usually count 3% of a credit card or line of credit balance as the payment, even if you pay less. Paying off a small loan before you apply, or not taking on a new car loan right before you buy, can make a real difference. Our post on what disqualifies you from a mortgage covers the other things that can stop a file cold.

Down Payment, Amortization and Other Things That Move the Number

The income you need isn't fixed. A few choices can change it a lot.

  • A bigger down payment. It doesn't change the income needed for a given loan size, but it shrinks the loan. Put more down and you need a smaller mortgage for the same home. With less than 20% down, a default insurance premium is added to the loan, which raises the amount you need to qualify for.
  • A 30-year amortization. Spreading payments over 30 years instead of 25 lowers the payment. On a $500,000 mortgage tested at 6.5%, the income needed drops from about $117,000 to about $110,000. First-time buyers and buyers of new builds can now get 30 years on an insured mortgage, and many uninsured mortgages allow it too. Our guide to the 30-year amortization for first-time buyers explains who qualifies.
  • Property tax and condo fees. These count inside GDS. A condo with a $500 monthly fee adds $250 to your housing costs on paper, which can take tens of thousands off what you qualify for.
  • Rental income. A legal suite can help. Lenders treat rental income in different ways, so how much it helps depends on the lender.

When Your Income Falls Short at a Bank

If the numbers above don't work at a bank, that's not the end of the road. Lenders fall into three broad groups, and each one looks at income differently.

  • Banks and credit unions (A-lenders) use the 39/44 limits and want income they can prove with pay stubs, a job letter, or two years of tax returns if you're self-employed. Some also offer programs for business owners that look past a low declared income.
  • Alternative lenders (B-lenders) can often go up to roughly 50% GDS and 50% TDS, depending on the file. That can mean a larger mortgage on the same income. They can also be more flexible on how income is shown, such as using about 12 months of business bank statements for self-employed borrowers. The trade-off is usually a higher rate, a lender fee, and a larger down payment.
  • Private lenders focus mostly on the property and your equity rather than your income. They usually want 20% to 25% down or more, and the cost is higher. They're best used as a short-term bridge while you fix what's holding you back.

There's no single right path. The best one depends on your income type, credit, down payment and plans. That's the part a broker works out with you.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much do you have to earn to qualify for a $200,000 mortgage?

Roughly $44,000 to $49,000 a year in household income, if you have no other debts and a 25-year amortization. Car loans, credit cards and condo fees raise that number. Your exact figure depends on the rate you're tested at and the lender's limits.

How much income do you need for a $500,000 mortgage in Canada?

Most people need roughly $105,000 to $117,000 a year in household income, with no other debts and a 25-year amortization. A 30-year amortization can lower that by several thousand dollars. Monthly debt payments can raise it quickly.

How much mortgage can I get with a $70,000 salary?

With no other debts, a $70,000 income may support a mortgage of roughly $290,000 to $330,000. Other monthly payments will lower that. Adding a co-borrower's income or choosing a longer amortization can raise it.

Do lenders use gross or net income for a mortgage?

Most lenders use your gross income, which is your pay before tax. Self-employed borrowers are often assessed on the income declared on their tax returns, though some lenders can use other ways to show income, such as business bank statements.

Can I qualify for a bigger mortgage with a broker?

Sometimes. A broker can't change the rules, but different lenders treat income and debts differently, and some allow higher debt ratios than a bank. A broker compares those options so you can see what you may qualify for across several lenders, not just one.
