When Is HELOC Interest Tax Deductible in Canada? The Use Rule Explained

You've built up equity in your home, and someone at a barbecue tells you to "use your HELOC and write off the interest." It sounds like free money. Then you hear the opposite from someone else: in Canada, you can't deduct interest on your home.

Both are partly right. Whether HELOC interest is tax deductible in Canada has almost nothing to do with the HELOC itself. It depends on what you spend the money on. Here's how the rule works, what usually qualifies, what doesn't, and how to set things up so your records hold up if the Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) ever asks.

Is HELOC Interest Tax Deductible? The Short Answer

Sometimes. In Canada, interest is generally deductible when you borrow money to earn income from a business or from property, such as a rental or an investment that pays income. That rule comes from paragraph 20(1)(c) of the Income Tax Act, and the CRA explains how it applies in its interest deductibility guidance.

The key word is use. The CRA looks at where the borrowed money actually went. It does not care what the loan is secured against. Its own guidance says the security for a loan has no impact on whether the interest is deductible. So a HELOC on your house can produce deductible interest, and a loan with no security at all can produce interest that isn't deductible.

This is why the "you can't deduct interest on your home" advice gets confused. It's true that the interest on the mortgage you used to buy the home you live in is not deductible in Canada. But if you borrow against that same home and put the money into a rental property, the interest on that borrowed amount can be.

The CRA also puts the job of proving it on you. You have to trace the borrowed dollars to the income-earning use. If you can't show where the money went, the deduction is hard to defend.

When HELOC Interest Can Be Tax Deductible

Here are the common uses where the interest on a HELOC draw can usually be deducted, as long as you can trace the money:

  • Buying a rental property. Using a HELOC for the down payment or the full purchase of a property you rent out. The interest on that portion is generally claimed against your rental income.
  • Improving a rental. Repairs or renovations on a property that earns rent.
  • Funding a business. Equipment, inventory or operating costs for a business you run. Self-employed borrowers usually claim this on their business statement.
  • Non-registered investments that can pay income. Interest-bearing investments, or shares where there's a reasonable expectation of dividends. The CRA generally accepts that common shares meet this test, unless the company has said it won't pay dividends.

Here's a simple example. Say you draw $80,000 from your HELOC and use all of it as the down payment on a rental condo. The interest you pay on that $80,000 is generally deductible against the rent. At an example rate of 5%, that's about $4,000 a year in interest you may be able to claim. The exact tax saving depends on your tax bracket and the rest of your return.

When It's Not Deductible

Most of the ways people actually use a HELOC are personal. Interest on those draws is not deductible:

  • Renovating the home you live in
  • Buying a car, paying for a wedding or taking a trip
  • Paying off credit cards or other personal debt
  • Contributing to an RRSP, TFSA or FHSA. The CRA says interest on money borrowed to contribute to any of these accounts can't be deducted.
  • Investments with no reasonable expectation of income, such as shares in a company that has said it won't pay dividends

Using a HELOC to consolidate high-interest debt can still be a smart move, because it often cuts your monthly payments a lot. It just won't give you a tax deduction.

Watch what happens after you sell, too. If you sell a rental or an investment and spend the money on something personal, the interest on that part of the loan usually stops being deductible. If you put the money into another income-earning investment, the deduction can generally carry on.

Mixed Use: How the CRA Splits HELOC Interest

A lot of people use one HELOC for everything. A rental down payment one month, a new furnace the next, a family trip in the summer. That's where it gets messy.

When a credit line is used for both eligible and personal purposes, the CRA's approach is to apply a fixed percentage to the balance. Its own example uses a $100,000 line where 40% went to an eligible use. When the borrower paid back $20,000, interest stayed deductible on 40% of the remaining $80,000, which is $32,000. The borrower could not choose to put the whole repayment toward the personal part.

In plain terms: once you mix the money, you lose control over which part you pay down first. You'd usually want to pay off the personal (non-deductible) part first and keep the deductible part. Mixing makes that much harder.

HELOC vs Home Equity Loan: Does the Type Change the Tax Answer?

No. A HELOC is a revolving line you can draw on and repay. A home equity loan is usually a lump sum, often set up as a refinance or a second mortgage. They work very differently day to day, but the tax test is the same for both: what did you use the money for?

If you take a lump-sum second mortgage and use it to buy a rental, that interest can be deductible. If you take a HELOC and use it for a kitchen in your own home, it isn't. If you're still choosing between the options, our guide to a HELOC, refinance or second mortgage walks through how each one works and what it costs.

Where you can get the money also varies by file. Most banks and credit unions offer HELOCs, usually with the revolving part capped at 65% of your home's value and up to 80% when combined with your mortgage. Some alternative (B) lenders also offer HELOCs, which can help when your income or credit doesn't fit a bank's box. Private lenders usually offer a lump-sum second mortgage rather than a revolving line, often for shorter terms. A broker can tell you which path fits your situation.

How to Set Up a HELOC So the Interest Stays Deductible

Good records are what make the deduction hold up. These habits help:

  1. Use a separate line or sub-account for investing. Many HELOCs can be split into separate segments. Keep one only for income-earning uses and never touch it for personal spending.
  2. Send the money straight to the investment. Pay the seller's lawyer, the contractor on the rental, or your investment account directly from the HELOC. Avoid moving it through your everyday chequing account first.
  3. Keep the paper trail. Save HELOC statements, purchase documents and transfer confirmations. If the CRA asks, you need to show each dollar's path.
  4. Don't pay personal bills from the investment line. Even one small personal charge mixes the account.
  5. Talk to your accountant first. Some people go further and slowly convert their regular mortgage into deductible investment debt. It can work, but it adds risk and needs careful tax advice.

Remember the risk side too. A HELOC is secured by your home. If the investment drops in value or the rental sits empty, you still owe the payments. A tax deduction lowers the cost of borrowing. It doesn't remove the risk. If you're buying a rental, our investment property mortgage page explains how lenders look at rental income.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is HELOC interest tax deductible in Canada?

It can be, depending on how you use the money. Interest is generally deductible when the borrowed money is used to earn income from a business or property, such as a rental or an income-paying investment. Interest on money used for personal things, like your own home renovation, is not deductible.

Can I deduct HELOC interest if I use it to buy a rental property?

Generally, yes. If you use a HELOC draw for the down payment or purchase of a rental, the interest on that amount is usually deductible against your rental income. Keep records that trace the money from the HELOC to the purchase.

Is interest on a home equity loan tax deductible in Canada?

The same rule applies as for a HELOC. The CRA looks at how you used the money, not the type of loan or what secures it. A lump-sum loan used for an income-earning purpose can have deductible interest. One used for personal spending does not.

Can I deduct interest if I borrow to contribute to my RRSP, TFSA or FHSA?

No. The CRA says interest on money borrowed to contribute to an RRSP, TFSA or FHSA is not deductible. Borrowing for those accounts can still make sense in some cases, but there's no interest write-off.

Is mortgage interest on my own home tax deductible in Canada?

No. Interest on the mortgage for the home you live in is generally not deductible in Canada. The exception is when you borrow against your home and use that money to earn income, such as buying a rental or funding a business.
