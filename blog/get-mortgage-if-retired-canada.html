Getting a Mortgage If You're Retired: How Lenders See Your Pension

You've paid down most of your house. You have a pension, CPP, OAS and some savings. Now you want to downsize, buy a condo closer to the grandkids, or pull some equity out for a renovation. Then someone at the bank asks, "Where do you work?" and the whole thing suddenly feels harder than it should.

So, can you get a mortgage if you're retired? Yes, in many cases you can. Lenders don't care that you've stopped working. They care whether your retirement income, your credit and your equity support the mortgage you're asking for. Can You Get a Mortgage If You're Retired?

Your age on its own isn't a reason to say no. Human rights laws in Canada protect you from being turned down just because of how old you are. There's no maximum age for a mortgage. A 72-year-old can sign a 25-year mortgage, the same as a 32-year-old.

What lenders do look at is the same list they use for everyone:

  • Your income, and whether it's steady and likely to keep coming.
  • Your debts, including car loans, credit cards and lines of credit.
  • Your credit history. Many retirees have spotless credit, which helps a lot.
  • Your down payment or equity, which is often the strongest part of a retired borrower's file.

You also still have to pass the mortgage stress test on most new mortgages and refinances. That means qualifying at a rate higher than the one you'll actually pay. As of 2026, that's the greater of 5.25% or your contract rate plus 2%. This is the part that catches many retirees, because a fixed pension has to cover a payment worked out at that higher rate.

What Retirement Income Lenders Count

Most steady retirement income counts toward a mortgage. Lenders treat some types a bit differently, so it helps to know how each one is usually looked at.

  • CPP and OAS. These are paid for life, so lenders like them. You usually prove them with your T4A(P) and T4A(OAS) slips, your Notice of Assessment, or recent bank statements that show the deposits.
  • Employer or private pensions. A defined benefit pension is about as solid as income gets. Lenders want to see that it's permanent and doesn't need to be re-approved. A pension statement, a T4A and a few months of deposits usually do the job.
  • RRIF withdrawals, annuities and investment income. Lenders often average this over two years and want to see that your savings can keep paying it. Capital gains usually don't count, because they aren't regular.
  • Rental income. If you own a rental or have a basement suite, part of that rent can usually be used. How much depends on the lender.
  • Part-time or contract work. Lots of retirees still work a bit. Lenders usually want to see some history with it, often two years.

Some non-taxable income, like the Guaranteed Income Supplement, can be "grossed up" by some lenders. That means they add a percentage on top because you don't pay tax on it. CPP and OAS are taxable, so they usually aren't grossed up. Rules on this vary a lot, which is one reason it pays to have someone check which lender treats your income best.

Why Retired Borrowers Get Turned Down (and What Helps)

The most common problem isn't credit. It's the size of the income. When you stop working, your income on paper often drops by a third or more, even if you have plenty of money in the bank. Lenders measure your mortgage against your income using two ratios:

  • GDS, your housing costs (mortgage, property tax, heat, and half of any condo fees) as a share of gross income.
  • TDS, your housing costs plus all other debt payments as a share of gross income.

Most banks and credit unions cap these at about 39% and 44%. If your pension is $60,000 a year, that sets a ceiling on the payment you can carry, no matter how much equity you have. Our guide on how much income you need for a mortgage shows how the numbers work at different mortgage sizes.

A few things often help a retired file get over the line:

  1. Pay off small debts first. At stress test rates, a $500 monthly car payment can cut what you qualify for by roughly $80,000. Clearing it before you apply can change everything.
  2. Put more down. If you're downsizing, using more of your sale proceeds as a down payment shrinks the mortgage and the payment.
  3. Show every income source. People forget the small pension from a job they had 20 years ago, or the RRIF they haven't started drawing from. It all adds up.
  4. Add a co-borrower. A spouse with their own pension, or an adult child who's working, can strengthen the file. Just know that everyone on the mortgage is fully responsible for it. Our post on intergenerational mortgages covers how families set this up.

Mortgage Options When You're Retired and Your Pension Isn't Enough

If a standard bank mortgage doesn't fit, that's not the end. There's a wide range of lenders, and each one looks at retirees in a different way. Here's the general picture.

  • Asset-based or net worth programs. Some banks and lenders will look past your income if you have a large amount in savings and investments on top of your down payment. The idea is simple: your money in the bank can cover the gap your pension leaves. These programs usually need strong credit and a meaningful amount in liquid assets, and the rules differ from lender to lender.
  • Alternative (B) lenders. B-lenders can allow debt ratios up to roughly 50% GDS and TDS, compared with the 39% and 44% most banks use. They can also work with credit scores below 600, depending on the overall file. Rates and fees are higher, so this is often a plan for a term or two.
  • Private lenders. Private lenders focus mostly on your equity, usually wanting you to keep at least 20% to 25% or more of the home's value. Terms are short, often one year, and costs are higher. It can work as a bridge, for example while you wait for your old house to sell.
  • A reverse mortgage. If you're 55 or older, a reverse mortgage lets you borrow against your home without making regular payments and without having to qualify on income. According to the Financial Consumer Agency of Canada, you can usually borrow up to 55% of your home's value. The trade-offs are real. Rates are usually higher than a regular mortgage, interest is added to the balance so the amount you owe grows over time, and less equity may be left for your estate. Our post on reverse mortgages in a gray divorce walks through how that growth adds up.

A home equity line of credit is another option people ask about. Keep in mind that a HELOC still has to be qualified on your income, just like a regular mortgage. It's not a way around the income rules.

Buying, Refinancing or Renewing a Mortgage in Retirement

What you're trying to do matters as much as your income. Here's how the main situations usually play out.

Renewing with your current lender. This is often the easiest. If you renew the same balance with the lender you already have, you usually don't have to qualify again, even if you've retired since you got the mortgage.

Switching lenders at renewal. Since November 21, 2024, a straight switch with the same balance and amortization doesn't have to pass the stress test. But the new lender will still look at your income as it is today, which means your pension, not your old salary. Our post on switching lenders at renewal with no stress test explains the details.

Refinancing. Taking out equity is a new mortgage. You'll need to qualify on your retirement income and pass the stress test. Most lenders cap a refinance at 80% of your home's value. If you're refinancing to pay off debt or help family, see how it works on our mortgage refinancing page.

Downsizing. Many retirees buy their next home before they sell the old one. If your sale is firm, bridge financing can cover the gap between the two closing dates. If you're buying with cash from the sale, you may not need a mortgage at all, but it's worth checking before you assume.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can you get a mortgage if you're retired?

Yes, in many cases. Lenders can't turn you down because of your age alone. They look at your retirement income, debts, credit and equity, and you still need to pass the stress test on most new mortgages and refinances.

Is there a maximum age to get a mortgage in Canada?

No. There's no maximum age for a mortgage in Canada, and a lender can't refuse you just because you're older. What matters is whether your income, credit and equity support the mortgage you want.

Does CPP and OAS count as income for a mortgage?

Yes. Most lenders count CPP and OAS as steady income because they're paid for life. You can usually prove them with your T4A slips, your Notice of Assessment, or bank statements showing the deposits.

Can I use my RRSP or RRIF to qualify for a mortgage?

Often, yes. Regular RRIF withdrawals can count as income, usually averaged over two years. Some lenders also have net worth programs that look at your savings and investments when your income alone isn't enough.

Is a reverse mortgage better than a regular mortgage when you're retired?

It depends. A reverse mortgage doesn't need income to qualify or regular payments, but rates are usually higher and the balance grows over time. If you can qualify for a regular mortgage, it often costs less. Compare both before you decide.
