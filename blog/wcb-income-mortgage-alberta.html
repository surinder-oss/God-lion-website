Qualifying for a Mortgage on Workers' Compensation Income in Canada

Book a Free Consultation

Pick a time that works for you — no obligation

Workers' compensation income can trip up a mortgage application. Not because it's bad income, but because many lender underwriters only see it a few times a year. The borrower has steady, documented payments from a provincial workers' compensation board, like WCB in Alberta. The deposits show up every month. The debt ratios work. And the file still stalls, often because it went to a lender that doesn't see much WCB income.

The good news: long-term WCB benefits can be some of the cleanest non-job income on a mortgage file. The payments come from a government-backed board, the terms are in writing, and they show up in your bank account and on your tax return. Most of the work is matching the file to the right lender with the right documents.

Quick answer: can you get a mortgage on WCB income?

  • Long-term monthly benefits (in Alberta, usually an Economic Loss Payment, or an older permanent disability pension) can often be used as qualifying income, much like a pension.
  • Short-term wage-loss benefits (temporary disability while you recover) are much harder to qualify on alone, because lenders can't tell how long they'll last.
  • A one-time lump sum (in Alberta, a Non-Economic Loss Payment) isn't income, but it may be used toward a down payment.
  • Because WCB benefits aren't taxed, many lenders "gross up" the amount, which can help you qualify for more.

Short-Term vs Long-Term WCB Benefits: How Lenders See Each

The single biggest question a lender asks about WCB income is: how long will it last? Here's how that usually plays out.

Type of benefitWhat it isHow lenders often treat it
Short-term wage loss (temporary disability, temporary partial disability)Paid while you recover and can't do your normal jobHard to use alone. Lenders usually want a return-to-work date, a letter from your employer holding your job, or other income that qualifies on its own.
Benefits during retraining (temporary economic loss)Paid while you take part in a WCB return-to-work or training planTreated as temporary. A written plan with an end date helps, but most lenders still want other stable income.
Long-term monthly benefits (Economic Loss Payment, older permanent disability pensions)Ongoing monthly payment when a permanent injury limits what you can earnOften accepted much like pension income, with proof of the amount and that it's ongoing.
Lump sum (Non-Economic Loss Payment)One-time payment for a permanent impairmentNot income. It may be used as a down payment or savings, with the decision letter and deposit record.

Your WCB decision letter will say which benefit you're on. That letter is the starting point for every WCB mortgage file.

Alberta WCB Benefit Types, in Plain English

WCB Alberta uses several benefit names. These are the ones that come up most on mortgage files:

Temporary disability (TD) and temporary partial disability (TPD). Wage-loss benefits while you recover. They can last a few weeks or much longer. They usually end when you go back to work, when WCB finds you fit for other work, or when the claim moves to a permanent benefit. Because the end date isn't known, these are the hardest WCB benefits to qualify on alone.

Temporary economic loss (TEL). Paid while you take part in a WCB vocational plan. Lenders treat it as temporary.

Economic Loss Payment (ELP). This is the main long-term benefit. If a permanent injury keeps you from earning what you did before, WCB pays a monthly amount based on 90% of the gap between your net earnings before the injury and what you can earn now. It's generally paid to age 65, then changes to a smaller ELP retirement benefit. WCB can review it, for example if your earnings change.

Non-Economic Loss Payment (NELP). A lump sum based on your permanent clinical impairment rating. It's paid once, so lenders won't count it as monthly income. It can still help as a down payment.

Older claims. If your claim is older, your letters may use terms like permanent partial disability (PPD) or permanent total disability (PTD). Lenders look at the same things: how much, how often, and for how long.

Outside Alberta, boards like WSIB in Ontario or WorkSafeBC use different names, but lenders ask the same question about every one of them: is this short-term or long-term?

Why Age Matters on a Long-Term WCB File

This one surprises people. Because an ELP is generally paid to age 65 and then drops to a smaller retirement amount, a lender may look at how close you are to 65. If you're 45, the payment runs for a long time and is easy to count. If you're 62, some lenders will want to see what your income looks like after 65, like the ELP retirement benefit, CPP, OAS or a pension, before they count the full amount. Bring your WCB letters that explain what happens at 65.

WCB and Your Tax Return

WCB benefits are not taxable, but they still show up on your tax return. You get a T5007 slip each year. The amount in box 10 goes on line 14400 as income, then the same amount is deducted on line 25000, so you don't pay tax on it.

That's handy for a mortgage. Your T5007 slips and Notice of Assessment give the lender an official record of what you received each year. It also proves the income isn't taxed, which is what lets many lenders gross it up.

How the Gross-Up Works

Lenders compare your income to your debts using gross (before-tax) income. A tax-free dollar of WCB goes further than a taxed dollar of pay, so many lenders "gross up" non-taxable income to level things out.

  • Many lenders gross up non-taxable income, often by up to about 25%.
  • Some use a smaller gross-up, or tie it to your tax bracket.
  • A few don't gross up at all, which means a smaller qualifying amount.

Example: you receive $3,000 a month in long-term WCB benefits.

  • With a 15% gross-up, the lender counts $3,450 a month ($41,400 a year)
  • With a 25% gross-up, the lender counts $3,750 a month ($45,000 a year)
  • With no gross-up, the lender counts $3,000 a month ($36,000 a year)

Same person, same income, but a different mortgage amount depending on the lender. This is one of the clearest cases where having many lenders to choose from matters.

WCB Mortgage Document Checklist

Getting these together early keeps the file moving:

  • WCB benefit confirmation letter. It should show the amount, how often it's paid, the benefit type and how long it's expected to last. Ask WCB for one that states the duration clearly.
  • WCB decision letter. The letter that set up your benefit and names the type (TD, TPD, TEL, ELP or an older pension).
  • Review schedule, if any. If your ELP is set for a review, include the letter that explains it.
  • T5007 slips for the last two years. These show what WCB paid you each year.
  • Notices of Assessment (and tax returns if the lender asks) for the last two years.
  • Bank statements for the last few months showing the WCB deposits.
  • Photo ID.
  • Job letter and pay stubs if you also work, including modified or part-time work.
  • Lump sum records if you're using a NELP for the down payment: the payment letter and the deposit in your account.
  • Age 65 details if you're close to 65: what your WCB benefit and other income will be after that.

If you also get CPP disability, a pension or other income, each one needs its own paperwork on top of this list.

How Different Lenders Handle WCB Income

Lender comfort with WCB income varies a lot. In general terms:

  • A-lenders (banks, credit unions and monoline lenders) often accept long-term WCB benefits with good documents. Gross-up rules and how they treat age and reviews differ from one to the next. Some are much more familiar with WCB files than others.
  • B-lenders can be more flexible when the file is harder, like a benefit with an unclear end date, mixed income, or bruised credit. They usually cost more and may need a bigger down payment. See our B-lender guide.
  • Private lenders focus on the property and your equity. They usually want 20% to 25% or more down and cost the most. They're often a short bridge while you build more payment history, with a plan to move to a regular lender later. See our private lender guide.

The right fit depends on your benefit type, how long it lasts, the gross-up, any other income, and your debt ratios. That's what we sort out before a file goes in.

Common Reasons WCB Files Get Declined (and How to Avoid Them)

  • Treating a short-term benefit like a permanent one. Temporary wage-loss benefits need backup, like an employer letter, a return-to-work plan, or other income. Without it, a decline is likely.
  • No duration on the letter. If the WCB letter doesn't say how long the benefit lasts, the lender may assume the worst. Ask for one that does.
  • Wrong lender. A lender with little WCB experience may decline a file another lender would approve.
  • Missing paperwork on other income. If WCB is combined with a job, self-employment or other benefits, each one needs full documents.
  • Credit problems from the injury period. A serious injury can lead to missed payments or high card balances. That needs its own plan. Our bad credit mortgage page and credit score guide cover the options.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can you qualify for a mortgage on WCB income?

Often, yes, depending on the benefit. Long-term monthly WCB benefits, like an Economic Loss Payment in Alberta, can often be used much like pension income if you can show the amount, how often it's paid and that it's ongoing. Short-term wage-loss benefits are much harder to use alone. Options may be available through A-lenders, B-lenders and private lenders depending on the full file.

How do lenders treat short-term vs long-term WCB benefits?

Short-term wage-loss benefits usually aren't enough on their own, because the end date isn't known. Lenders often want a return-to-work date, an employer letter holding your job, or other income. Long-term monthly benefits are treated more like a pension. A one-time lump sum isn't income, but it may help with a down payment.

What documents do I need for a WCB income mortgage?

Usually a WCB benefit confirmation letter showing the amount, frequency, benefit type and duration; the WCB decision letter; T5007 slips and Notices of Assessment for the last two years; recent bank statements showing the deposits; photo ID; and paperwork for any other income. If you're close to 65, bring details on what your income will be after 65.

Is WCB income grossed up for a mortgage?

Often, yes. WCB benefits aren't taxable, so many lenders gross them up (add a percentage on top) to compare them fairly with taxed income. Some use a smaller gross-up and a few don't gross up at all. The same income can qualify for different amounts at different lenders.

Does WCB income show on my tax return?

Yes, even though it isn't taxed. WCB sends a T5007 slip. The box 10 amount goes on line 14400 as income and is deducted again on line 25000. Your T5007 slips and Notice of Assessment give lenders an official record of what you received.

Can I use a WCB lump sum as a down payment?

In many cases, yes. A lump sum like a Non-Economic Loss Payment isn't counted as income, but it can be used toward a down payment or closing costs. Lenders will want the WCB letter showing the payment and the bank record of the deposit.

What happens to my WCB benefit at 65, and does it affect my mortgage?

In Alberta, an Economic Loss Payment is generally paid to age 65 and then changes to a smaller ELP retirement benefit. If you're close to 65, some lenders will look at your income after 65 before counting the full amount. Bring the WCB letters that explain your benefit at 65, plus any CPP, OAS or pension details.

Can I get a mortgage while on a temporary WCB claim?

It depends on the timeline and your other income. Lenders rarely qualify someone on temporary WCB benefits alone. It helps to have an employer letter confirming your job and pay are being held, a WCB return-to-work plan, or a spouse or other income that qualifies on its own. Sometimes the smarter move is to wait until you're back at work or your benefit becomes long-term.
