What Disqualifies You From a Mortgage? (And What Only Stops You at a Bank)

A Bank's No Is Not Canada's No

Canada has three main groups of mortgage lenders, and each one plays by different rules.

  • A-lenders: the big banks, credit unions and monoline lenders. They usually cost the least, but they have the tightest rules on credit, income and debt.
  • B-lenders (alternative lenders): they look past a bruised credit score, newer self-employment, or higher debt. The rate and fees are higher.
  • Private lenders: they care most about your home's equity and your plan to move to a cheaper lender later. They're the most flexible and the most expensive, and usually short term.

When a bank declines you, it's telling you that you don't fit its box. It isn't telling you that you can't get a mortgage. The real question is which box your file fits today, and what it would take to move up to a cheaper one.

What Disqualifies You From a Mortgage Almost Everywhere

A few things will stop a file at nearly every lender, including the flexible ones. These are the real dealbreakers.

  1. Mortgage fraud or a false application. Fake pay stubs, a made-up job letter, hiding debts, or saying you'll live in a home you plan to rent out. Lenders check, and if they find it, it ends the file. It can also follow you.
  2. No down payment you can prove. Every lender needs to see where your down payment came from. Money that showed up in your account last week with no paper trail is a problem. Gifts from family usually work, but they need a signed gift letter and a clear trail.
  3. No way to pay the mortgage and no equity to fall back on. A B-lender can stretch your debt ratios. A private lender can lean on equity. But if there's no income to carry the payment and not enough equity, there's no path.
  4. A property no lender will finance. Some homes are hard to lend on: serious structural damage, certain unusual builds, or properties with legal or zoning problems. Sometimes it's the house that gets declined, not you.
  5. An undischarged bankruptcy, for most purchases. While you're still in an active bankruptcy, buying a home is very hard. Once you're discharged, the doors start to open again.

If none of these describe you, you are probably not "unmortgageable." You likely just need a different lender, a little time, or a better plan.

What Only Disqualifies You at a Bank

This is the list most people never hear. Each one can lead to a no at a bank, but other lenders may say yes, depending on the full file.

A credit score below the bank's cutoff

For an insured mortgage (less than 20% down), at least one borrower needs a score of 600 or higher, according to CMHC's borrower rules. Many bank products want 680 or more. B-lenders can go below 600, depending on the overall file, and private lenders focus on equity more than the score. Our guide to the minimum credit score for a mortgage explains each tier.

A recent consumer proposal or bankruptcy

Most banks want these well in the past, often with re-built credit since. B-lenders can work with clients who've recently been discharged or have finished, or are still paying, a consumer proposal. If you're in one now, read our guide to getting a mortgage during or after a consumer proposal, or our post on buying a home after bankruptcy in Alberta.

Short self-employment history

Banks usually want two years of tax returns and qualify you on the average of your declared income. If you've been on your own for one year, or you write off a lot, the bank's number may be too low. Some A-lenders have stated-income programs. Many B-lenders can use about 12 months of business bank statements instead of your latest Notice of Assessment. Private lenders look at equity.

Income that's new, variable or hard to show

Being on probation at a new job, recent commission or contract income, or income from outside Canada can all trip up a bank. Some A-lenders can work with these, and B-lenders have more room. The paperwork matters more than the job title.

Debt ratios over the bank limit

This is the most common reason for a decline, and we'll cover it next.

Debt Ratios: The Real Dealbreaker at a Bank

Lenders use two numbers to decide what you can afford.

  • GDS (Gross Debt Service): your mortgage payment, property tax, heating and half of any condo fees, divided by your gross monthly income.
  • TDS (Total Debt Service): all of that, plus your other debts like car loans, credit card minimums and lines of credit.

For an insured mortgage, CMHC caps these at 39% GDS and 44% TDS. Many banks use the same limits on other mortgages too.

There's a catch. You aren't tested at your actual rate. Banks and other federally regulated lenders must qualify you at the higher of your contract rate plus 2%, or 5.25%. That's the "stress test," set out in OSFI's minimum qualifying rate for uninsured mortgages, and CMHC uses the same test for insured ones. It's why someone who can easily make the real payment still gets declined on paper.

Here's how it can play out. A couple earns $110,000 a year together. They have a $550 car payment and $400 in credit card minimums. On paper, with the stress test, their TDS lands at 48%. The bank says no. A B-lender that allows debt ratios up to roughly 50% may say yes, at a higher cost. Or, if they pay off the credit cards first, their ratio may drop under the bank's 44% limit. Same people, same income, three different outcomes.

What Looks Bad on Your Bank Statements

Lenders usually ask for 90 days of statements for your down payment account. They read them closely. These are the things that raise questions:

  • NSF charges or bounced payments. One may be explained. A pattern looks like trouble managing money.
  • Large deposits you can't explain. Lenders need to know where every big deposit came from. Keep receipts if you sold a car or got a bonus.
  • Payments to lenders that aren't on your credit report. A private loan, a buy-now-pay-later plan or a payday loan shows up here, and the lender will count it.
  • Regular overdraft use. Living in the overdraft month after month tells a lender your budget is tight.
  • Moving money around just before you apply. Money that bounces between accounts with no clear story slows everything down.

None of these is an automatic no. But each one needs an answer, and it's much easier to give one before the file goes in than after.

What to Do If You've Been Declined

  1. Ask why, and get it in writing if you can. "Debt ratio too high" and "credit score too low" need very different fixes.
  2. Pull your own credit report. You can get it free from Equifax and TransUnion. Look for errors, old accounts that should be closed, and collections you didn't know about.
  3. Don't apply at five more banks. Each new application can add a credit check. A broker can show your file to many lenders with one.
  4. Look at the debt side. Paying down a card or a car loan can do more for your approval than a raise.
  5. Think about the bridge. If a B-lender or private lender is the path today, go in with a plan to move to a bank-level mortgage at renewal, once your credit or income has caught up.

Frequently Asked Questions

What disqualifies you from a mortgage in Canada?

The hard stops at almost every lender are fraud, a down payment you can't prove, no income or equity to carry the payment, a property no lender will finance, and an active bankruptcy. Most other problems, like a low score or high debt ratios, only disqualify you at a bank. Alternative and private lenders may still have options.

Can I get a mortgage with horrible credit?

Sometimes, yes. Banks usually want a score in the high 600s, and insured mortgages need at least one borrower at 600 or higher. Alternative lenders can look at scores below 600 depending on the whole file, and private lenders focus on your equity. Expect a higher rate and fees, and plan to move to a cheaper lender once your credit improves.

What is the most common reason for a mortgage being declined?

Debt ratios are one of the most common reasons. Lenders test you at a higher rate than you'll pay, so car loans and credit card payments can push your total debt ratio over the bank's 44% limit. Paying down debt before you apply often helps more than people expect.

Does being declined for a mortgage hurt your credit?

The decline itself doesn't show up on your credit report. The credit check that came with the application does, and several checks in a short time can lower your score a little. That's why it's better to have a broker show your file to several lenders than to apply at bank after bank on your own.

How long after a consumer proposal can I get a mortgage?

It depends on the lender. Many banks want the proposal finished and some time of re-built credit after it. Some alternative lenders can work with you while you're still paying it, or soon after you finish. A broker can tell you which path fits your timeline.
