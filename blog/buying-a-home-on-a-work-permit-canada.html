Buying a Home in Canada on a Work Permit: The Two Gates You Have to Clear

Book a Free Consultation

Pick a time that works for you — no obligation

You do not need PR to buy a home in Canada. You need a work permit with at least 183 days left, a down payment you can trace, and a lender that is comfortable with your file.

That question comes up in almost every first call we take from a newcomer family: am I even allowed to buy yet, or do I have to wait for my PR? Buying a home in Canada on a work permit is allowed, and thousands of people do it every year without permanent residence. What trips families up is that there are two separate gates, and most people only ever hear about one. The first gate is the law: whether a non-Canadian is permitted to buy that particular property at all. The second is the lender: whether anyone will actually fund it. You have to clear both. Clearing one and assuming the other will sort itself out is how good buyers lose houses.

Gate One: Is a Work Permit Holder Allowed to Buy?

Since the start of 2023 there has been a federal rule that stops most non-Canadians from buying homes here. It is called the Prohibition on the Purchase of Residential Property by Non-Canadians Act. People just call it the foreign buyer ban.

It is narrower than the name suggests, and there is a carve-out written specifically for people working here.

You are generally covered by that carve-out if you hold a valid work permit, or you are otherwise authorized to work in Canada, and two things are true. Your permit has at least 183 days of validity left on the day you buy. And you have not already bought more than one home in Canada under this rule. That is the whole test. There is no requirement to have PR, no requirement to have been here a certain number of years.

A few other things worth knowing about how the ban works:

  • It only bites in cities. The rule applies inside census metropolitan areas and census agglomerations. A property well outside one of those is not covered at all.
  • It only covers small residential properties — buildings with three dwelling units or fewer, which includes houses, semis and condo units. A building with four or more units is not covered, and vacant land has been outside the rule since March 2023.
  • Students have their own path. Study permit holders can buy, but the bar is much higher: enrolled at a designated learning institution, tax returns filed for each of the five years before the purchase, physically in Canada at least 244 days in each of those five years, a purchase price no higher than $500,000, and no more than one property.
  • Buying with a Canadian partner works. If your spouse or common-law partner is a citizen or a permanent resident and you are buying together, the ban does not stand in your way.
  • Getting it wrong is expensive. A breach can bring a fine of up to $10,000, and a court can order the property sold.
  • Provinces can add their own costs. The federal ban is separate from provincial taxes. Alberta has no foreign buyer tax. British Columbia does: in many areas, a buyer who is not a citizen or PR can owe an extra 20% property transfer tax. Our post on buying a home in Surrey walks through it.

One timing note, because it matters right now. As of October 2026, the ban is still set to end on January 1, 2027. Ottawa has said it is reviewing the rule, and the reporting points to a possible replacement rather than a simple renewal. But nothing has been tabled or passed yet. So treat the ban as live until it is not. If your closing date lands in early 2027, do not plan around the ban ending. Plan as if the 183-day test still applies, and have your real estate lawyer confirm the rules on the day you write the offer.

One more point people miss. Even if the federal ban ends, the lender rules in the next section do not change with it. The ban decides whether you are allowed to buy. The lender decides whether anyone will fund it. You can read the Act itself on the federal justice laws website.

Gate Two: What Lenders Want From a Work Permit File

Clearing the law is the easy half. The lender is where the file is actually won or lost, and this is where a bank's answer and the market's answer are often two different answers.

There is no single rule for what a work permit buyer needs down. It depends on which door you go through.

The insured route. If you are living and working here, buying a home you will live in, there are default-insured programs built for exactly your situation. Down payments on these can start as low as 5%, though some lenders and insurers ask for 10% depending on your Canadian credit and how long you have been here. Expect the home to be owner-occupied and the price to be under $1.5 million. Many of these programs also want to see that you arrived in Canada within the last five years and have at least three months of full-time work here. Some will not allow a guarantor.

The minimum down payment math is the same one everyone uses on an insured purchase: 5% of the first $500,000, then 10% of the part above $500,000. On a $600,000 home, that works out to $35,000. If this is your first home in Canada and the mortgage is insured, you may also be able to stretch the amortization to 30 years, which lowers the payment. The extra five years usually comes with a small added insurance premium, so it is worth running both numbers.

Where the money comes from matters too. At least 5% normally has to be your own savings. Some programs will let the rest come as a gift from immediate family, with a signed gift letter, and some will not. The gift has to be documented before closing, so ask your family to move it early.

The conventional route. Put 20% down and you are outside insurance rules and into a wider set of lenders. This is often the cleaner path if you have the funds, especially if your employment history here is short or your income is structured in a way an insured program does not like.

When the A-side says no. Big banks and credit unions usually qualify around 39% GDS and 44% TDS, and tighter than that if your credit score is on the low side. Alternative lenders — the B side — can stretch those ratios closer to 50/50 and can work with scores under 600 when the rest of the file supports it. Private lenders are equity-driven and the most flexible of all on income and status, and they usually want 20% to 25% down or more. Those are three real doors, not one. Being on a work permit does not automatically push you to the back of the line.

Does Your Type of Work Permit Matter?

Yes, but not in the way most people think. Lenders do not have one list of "good" and "bad" permits. What they look at is how stable your income looks and how much time is left on your status.

  • Employer-specific permit. Your permit is tied to one employer. Lenders like this when the job is full-time and the employer letter is strong. The risk they see is that a job change means a new permit, so they look closely at how long you have been in the role.
  • Open work permit. You can work for almost any employer. This includes many spousal open work permits and post-graduation work permits. The permit itself is fine. The lender will focus on your actual job: full-time or part-time, permanent or contract, and whether you are past probation.
  • Time left on the permit. The law needs 183 days. Some lenders want more than that, and some want to see a history of the permit being renewed. This is one of the biggest reasons two lenders give the same buyer two different answers.
  • PR in progress. A PR application on its own does not replace a valid permit. If your permit is close to expiry, lenders want proof the renewal was applied for. If you are on maintained status, say so on the first call so we can match you to a lender that accepts it.

The Documents to Have Ready

The list is longer than a citizen's file, but none of it is hard to get. Pull these together before you start looking at homes:

  • Your work permit, with the issue date and expiry date clearly showing
  • Your passport
  • A letter of employment showing your job title, start date, salary or hourly rate, hours, and whether the job is permanent
  • Your two most recent pay stubs
  • 90 days of statements on the account holding your down payment, plus wire receipts for any money sent from overseas
  • Proof of credit history: your Canadian credit report if you have one, or one of the alternatives in the credit section below
  • If you have filed taxes here, your Notice of Assessment

The 90-day history on your down payment is the one that catches people out, and we will come back to it.

The Permit Expiry Date That Sinks Files

This is the single most useful thing in this article, so read this part twice.

Your work permit usually has to be valid on the advance date — the day the money actually moves to your lawyer — not just on the day you applied. A permit that comfortably covers your application and then expires two weeks before possession is a problem the lender will find, and they will find it late.

If your permit is going to expire in that window, most lenders will want to see the extension, plus evidence that your job is carrying on. That is workable. What is not workable is discovering it at the eleventh hour with a firm sale and a moving truck booked.

So do three things. Tell your broker your permit expiry date on the first call, before anyone starts shopping. Start your renewal early and keep the acknowledgement from immigration. And if you are buying a new build with a long completion date, say so up front, because a nine-month closing and a five-month permit do not go together without a plan.

No Canadian Credit Yet? Here Is What Takes Its Place

Most people who land here have no Canadian credit file for the first several months, and they assume that is fatal. It is not.

Insured newcomer and non-permanent-resident programs typically look for a Canadian credit score around 640. If you do not have one yet, lenders will accept alternative proof instead. That usually means an international credit bureau report from your home country, or twelve months of clean payment history on things like rent and utilities, or a reference letter from a recognized financial institution showing how you have handled accounts there. If you are putting down 10% or more, some programs will also accept six months of clean bank statements. The less you put down, the stronger the proof they want.

If you already have a Canadian score but it is low, that is a different conversation. Alternative lenders can work below 600 when the rest of the file is strong, and private lenders care far more about your down payment than your score.

The practical move is to start building here on day one anyway. A secured credit card used lightly and paid in full, a phone plan in your own name, rent paid from your own account by a traceable method. Six to twelve months of that changes what is available to you.

Can You Use an FHSA or RRSP on a Work Permit?

Often, yes. This surprises a lot of our clients. The test for these accounts is your tax residency, not your immigration status. If you live here, work here and file Canadian taxes as a resident, you can usually open a First Home Savings Account (FHSA) the same as a citizen can.

The FHSA lets you put in up to $8,000 a year, to a lifetime total of $40,000. You get a tax deduction going in, and the money comes out tax-free for your first home. You also have to be a first-time buyer. That means you have not lived in a home you or your spouse owned in this year or the four years before it. A home you owned and lived in back home counts against you here, so be upfront about it.

The RRSP Home Buyers' Plan works too, up to $60,000 each, but only if you have RRSP room. Room comes from Canadian earned income in past tax years, so someone who arrived last year may not have much yet. Our post on the FHSA and Home Buyers' Plan explains how to use both on the same purchase. Talk to your accountant before you open either one if your tax residency is not clear.

Living Outside Canada Is a Completely Different File

Do not confuse these two situations, because lenders certainly do not.

A work permit holder living and working here is a temporary resident, and the programs above are open. Someone living abroad who wants to buy here is a non-resident, and that file is much tighter. Expect a down payment around 35%, a full appraisal, a smaller pool of lenders, and in some cases a requirement to sign in person in Canada rather than at a consulate.

There is a trap worth naming here. At some lenders, if a non-resident applies jointly with a Canadian citizen or permanent resident, the whole application still gets read under the non-resident rules. The Canadian partner does not automatically rescue the file. That is a lender-by-lender question and exactly the kind of thing that decides where your application should go.

If your money is earned abroad while you live here, that is its own subject, and we cover it in what lenders accept as proof of foreign income.

Four Mistakes That Cost Work Permit Buyers a Home

Waiting for PR when you already qualify. We meet families every year who sat out two or three years of a market they could have bought into. If your permit has 183 days on it and your job is steady, there is usually a file to look at today.

Treating one bank's no as the country's answer. A single institution has one set of rules and one appetite for temporary residents. There are dozens of lenders in this country and they do not agree with each other about anything, least of all this. A no from one branch tells you about that branch.

Wiring the down payment in at the last minute. Money that lands from overseas three days before closing, with no history behind it, will hold up your file. Lenders need to see where it came from, usually 90 days of statements, and on a foreign transfer they will want the wire receipts showing the sender. Move the funds early and keep every piece of paper.

Skipping the pre-approval and shopping first. On a straightforward file that is a small mistake. On a work permit file, where status, credit and income all need matching to the right lender, it is a big one. Getting pre-approved before you shop is what tells you which houses are real for you.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I buy a house in Canada on a work permit?

Generally yes. The federal ban on non-Canadians buying residential property has an exception for people who hold a valid work permit or are otherwise authorized to work here, as long as the permit has at least 183 days of validity remaining at the time of purchase and you have not already bought more than one home under the rule. Permanent residence is not required. The ban also only applies inside census metropolitan areas and census agglomerations, so a property outside one falls outside the rule entirely.

How much down payment do I need to buy a home on a work permit?

It depends on the route. Default-insured programs for work permit holders can start as low as 5% down (5% of the first $500,000 and 10% of the part above), though some lenders and insurers ask for 10% depending on your Canadian credit history. Those programs are for owner-occupied homes priced under $1.5 million. Going conventional means 20% down and a wider set of lenders. Alternative lenders sit above that, and private lenders are usually 20% to 25% down or more. At least 5% normally has to come from your own savings with a 90-day paper trail, and some programs allow the rest as a gift from immediate family.

What happens if my work permit expires before my closing date?

Most lenders need the permit valid on the advance date, which is when funds are released to your lawyer, not just when you applied. If it expires before then, you will usually be asked for proof the extension has been applied for or granted, along with evidence that your employment is continuing. It is manageable when it is handled early and painful when it surfaces a week before possession, so give your broker the expiry date at the very start.

Do I need Canadian credit history to get a mortgage on a work permit?

Not necessarily. Insured newcomer programs commonly look for a Canadian score around 640, but where there is no Canadian file yet, lenders will consider alternatives such as an international credit bureau report, twelve months of documented rent and utility payments, or a reference letter from a recognized financial institution abroad. Building Canadian credit as soon as you arrive still widens your options considerably.

Can I buy a home in Canada if I live outside the country?

That is a different file. Someone living abroad is treated as a non-resident, which usually means around 35% down, a full appraisal, a much smaller group of lenders, and sometimes a requirement to sign in person in Canada. Note that at some lenders a joint application between a non-resident and a Canadian citizen or permanent resident is still underwritten under the non-resident rules, so having a Canadian co-applicant does not automatically change the terms.

Does it matter if I have an open or an employer-specific work permit?

Both can work. Lenders care less about the permit type and more about your job and your time left in Canada. With an employer-specific permit, they look at how long you have been with that employer. With an open permit, they look at whether your job is full-time, permanent and past probation. Some lenders also want more time left on the permit than the 183 days the law requires.

Can I open an FHSA on a work permit?

Usually, yes. FHSA eligibility depends on being a resident of Canada for tax purposes, being of age, and being a first-time buyer, not on having PR. You can put in up to $8,000 a year, to a lifetime total of $40,000. A home you owned and lived in outside Canada in the current year or the previous four years can make you ineligible, so check with your accountant if that applies to you.

Is the foreign buyer ban ending in 2027?

As of October 2026, the ban is set to end on January 1, 2027, and Ottawa has said it is reviewing the rule. No extension or replacement had been tabled. Until a change is official, plan as if the 183-day work permit test still applies, and have your real estate lawyer confirm the rules when you write your offer. The lender requirements for work permit buyers do not change either way.
