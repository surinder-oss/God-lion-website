What Does It Cost to Refinance a Mortgage in Alberta? (A $250,000 Example)

You've got $35,000 on credit cards and a car loan. You've built up equity in your home. Someone tells you to refinance and roll it all into the mortgage. Then the question hits: what's the real cost to refinance a mortgage, and will the savings actually beat it?

Most people only hear about the rate. The rate is not the whole bill. There are fees, there may be a penalty, and there's the qualifying test. Here's every cost in plain numbers, using a $250,000 mortgage in Alberta as the example, plus a simple way to tell if it's worth it.

What It Really Costs to Refinance a Mortgage

A refinance means you replace your current mortgage with a new one, usually to borrow more against your home or change the terms. Because it's a new mortgage, there's paperwork, and paperwork costs money. These are the usual fees in Alberta as of 2026:

  • Appraisal: about $300 to $600. The lender needs to know what your home is worth today. Some lenders use a computer valuation and skip this.
  • Legal fees: about $800 to $1,500. A lawyer handles the title search, pays out the old mortgage and registers the new one.
  • Land Titles registration: Alberta charges $50 plus $5 for every $5,000 of the mortgage. On $250,000, that's $300.
  • Discharge fee: about $200 to $400, charged by your old lender to remove its mortgage if you move to a new one.
  • Title insurance: about $200 to $400 on some files.

Add it up and most refinances land somewhere around $1,400 to $3,200 in fees. That's before the biggest cost on many files, which is the penalty.

Sometimes a lender will cover the legal or appraisal cost to win your business. And if your current mortgage is registered for more than you owe (some lenders do this, called a collateral charge), staying with the same lender can sometimes skip the new registration. Ask before you assume.

The Big One: Your Prepayment Penalty

If you refinance in the middle of your term, you're breaking your mortgage contract. Your lender will charge a prepayment penalty. If you refinance at renewal, there's no penalty at all.

How the penalty works depends on your mortgage type:

  • Variable rate: usually three months' interest. On a $250,000 balance at an example rate of 4.5%, that's about $2,800.
  • Fixed rate: usually the higher of three months' interest or the interest rate differential (IRD). The IRD is roughly the interest the lender loses because you're leaving early.

The IRD is where people get surprised. Here's a simple version. Say your rate is 1% higher than what the lender can lend at today for the time you have left, and you have two years left on a $250,000 balance. That's about 1% x $250,000 x 2 years, or $5,000. Real lender formulas are more complex, and some use posted rates that make the number bigger. Two people with the same balance can get very different penalties from different lenders.

The Financial Consumer Agency of Canada explains how lenders work out these penalties and notes that the method varies by lender. The only number you can trust is the one your lender gives you in writing. Our mortgage penalty guide walks through the math step by step.

Cost to Refinance a Mortgage of $250,000: A Worked Example

Let's put it together. You owe $250,000 on a variable-rate mortgage with a bank, and your home is worth $450,000. You want to pull out $35,000 to pay off high-interest debt, so the new mortgage will be $285,000.

  • Appraisal: $450
  • Legal fees: $1,100
  • Land Titles registration on $285,000: $335
  • Discharge fee from the old lender: $300
  • Penalty (three months' interest): about $2,800

Total: about $4,985.

If you were on a fixed rate with two or three years left, the penalty alone could be $5,000 to $10,000 or more, and the total could double. If you waited until renewal, you'd drop the penalty and the total would be closer to $2,200.

One more thing to know. At a bank or credit union, you can usually borrow up to 80% of your home's value on a refinance. On a $450,000 home that's $360,000, so this example fits with room to spare. Many lenders will let you add the costs into the new mortgage if you're under that limit, so you don't pay them out of pocket.

A-Lender, B-Lender or Private: How the Cost Changes

Not every refinance goes to a bank. Which lender fits your file changes the cost a lot.

Banks and credit unions (A-lenders)

The lowest cost, if you qualify. You'll usually pay the fees above and no lender fee. But you have to pass the stress test. For an uninsured mortgage, that means qualifying at your contract rate plus 2%, or 5.25%, whichever is higher (the federal rule as of 2026). Your credit and income need to be in good shape.

Alternative lenders (B-lenders)

B-lenders help when your credit has taken a hit, your income is harder to prove, or your debt ratios are high. They can allow debt ratios up to roughly 50% of your income, and some can work with scores below 600 depending on the whole file. The trade-off is a higher rate plus a lender fee, often around 1% to 2% of the mortgage. On $285,000, 1% is $2,850. There may also be a broker fee on these files, and we'll tell you the number up front.

Private lenders

Private lenders focus on your equity, not your credit score. They usually want you to keep 20% to 25% or more of your home's value as equity, and terms are short, often one year. Fees are higher again, often 2% to 3% or more, plus a broker fee. A private refinance makes sense as a short bridge, for example to pay off CRA debt or stop a collection, with a clear plan to move to a cheaper lender later.

None of these paths is the only one. The right one depends on your credit, income, equity and how fast you need the money.

Is Refinancing Worth It? How to Find Your Break-Even

Here's the simple test we use. Take your total cost and divide it by how much you save each month.

Total cost ÷ monthly savings = months to break even.

Example 1, debt consolidation. Your card and car payments add up to $1,250 a month. Rolling that $35,000 into the mortgage might add about $200 a month to your mortgage payment, depending on your rate and amortization. You save around $1,050 a month. With $4,985 in costs, you break even in about five months. That's a strong case.

One warning. Spreading car and card debt over 25 years can mean more interest overall, even with a lower rate. If you can, keep paying some of that freed-up money toward the mortgage. Our guide to refinancing to pay off debt in Alberta covers when it makes sense and when it doesn't.

Example 2, chasing a lower rate. You're on a fixed rate with three years left. A lower rate would save you $160 a month. But the IRD penalty is $7,500 and fees are $2,500, so your cost is $10,000. You break even in about 63 months, which is longer than the three years you had left. That's usually a no.

What about the "2% rule"?

You may have read that refinancing is worth it once rates drop by 2%. That's mostly an American rule of thumb. In Canada, most mortgages have closed terms and penalties, so a 2% drop can still lose you money if your IRD is big. And a refinance for debt can make sense even with no rate drop at all. Skip the rule and run your own break-even.

Ways to Lower What You Pay

  1. Refinance at renewal. No penalty. Start looking about four months before your maturity date, since many lenders let you lock in that early.
  2. Ask your current lender first. Some lenders will add money to your mortgage and blend your rate, which can avoid the penalty. It's not always the cheapest option, but get the number.
  3. Use your prepayment privilege first. Some lenders let you make your yearly lump-sum payment before they work out the penalty, which can shrink it. Ask how your lender handles this.
  4. Ask who pays the legal and appraisal. Some lenders cover these on certain refinances.
  5. Borrow only what you need. If you need a smaller amount, a HELOC or second mortgage may cost less than breaking your whole mortgage. See our guide to HELOC vs refinance vs second mortgage.

If you only want a better rate and don't need new money, wait for renewal. A straight switch to a new lender at renewal usually skips the stress test and most fees. Our post on refinancing vs switching your mortgage explains the difference.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does it cost to refinance a mortgage in Alberta?

Most refinances cost about $1,400 to $3,200 in fees, including the appraisal, legal fees, Land Titles registration and a discharge fee. If you break your term early, add a prepayment penalty, which can be a few thousand dollars or much more on a fixed rate. Alternative and private lenders also charge lender fees.

What does it cost to refinance a $250,000 mortgage?

Without a penalty, expect roughly $2,000 to $2,500 in fees at a bank or credit union. With a variable-rate penalty of three months' interest, the total could be around $5,000. With a fixed-rate IRD penalty, it can be more. Your lender's written penalty quote is the number that decides it.

What is the 2% rule for refinancing?

It's a rule of thumb that says refinance when rates fall by 2%. It mostly comes from the U.S. In Canada, penalties on closed mortgages can wipe out the savings, so it's better to divide your total cost by your monthly savings and see how long it takes to break even.

Can I add the refinance costs to my mortgage?

Often, yes. Many lenders let you include fees and even the penalty in the new mortgage, as long as you stay within their limit. At a bank or credit union, that's usually 80% of your home's value. You'll pay interest on those costs over time.

Is it cheaper to refinance at renewal?

Usually, yes. At renewal there's no prepayment penalty, which is often the largest cost. You'll still pay fees like the appraisal and legal work if you're adding money, but you avoid breaking your contract.
