Thinking of Waiving Your Financing Condition? What Happens If the Money Doesn't Arrive

The house is right. There are three other offers. Your realtor says the sellers want a clean offer with no conditions, and you have until 8 p.m. to decide.

You have a pre-approval, so waiving your financing condition feels safe. Most of the time the money does show up. But when it doesn't, the cost can be much bigger than the deposit, and most buyers only find that out after they have signed.

Here is what the financing condition protects, what happens if you waive it and the mortgage falls through, and how to lower the risk if you decide to go ahead.

What Your Financing Condition Actually Protects

In Alberta, most resale homes are bought on the standard Alberta Real Estate Association (AREA) Residential Purchase Contract. Other provinces use their own forms, but the idea is much the same. The clause numbers below come from the current Alberta form.

Clause 8.2(a) is the financing condition. It makes the deal depend on you getting a new mortgage, from a lender you choose, on terms you are happy with, by a set date called the Condition Day. It also records the down payment you plan to make.

Clause 8.4 sets the rules for ending it. By the Condition Day, you give the seller written notice that the condition is waived or satisfied. If you don't, the contract ends. Under clause 4.8, if a condition is not met or waived in time, your deposit goes back to you.

So while the condition is in place, you have a way out if the lender says no. It isn't a free exit if you simply change your mind, because clause 8.1 says you have to make a reasonable, good-faith effort to meet your own conditions. But it is real protection if the financing doesn't come through. The moment you waive it, that protection is gone.

What Happens If You Waive Your Financing Condition and the Money Doesn't Arrive

Once every condition is waived or satisfied, the deal is firm. If you then can't close, you are the one who broke the contract. Clause 3.1(c) of the Alberta form says time is of the essence, which means the dates are strictly enforced. Here is what that can cost you.

  • Your deposit. Under clause 4.8, if all conditions are satisfied or waived and the buyer fails to close, the deposit goes to the seller.
  • More than the deposit. Clause 4.9 says paying out the deposit doesn't stop the seller from using other remedies. Clause 12.1 lets them claim damages, plus reasonable legal costs on a full indemnity basis.
  • The price gap on resale. If the seller relists and sells for less, that shortfall is the most common claim. Say you agreed to pay $550,000 and the home later sells for $520,000. The seller may claim the $30,000, plus costs like property tax, utilities, insurance and condo fees while the home sat unsold. A seller does have to take reasonable steps to limit the loss, but that doesn't make the bill small.
  • Late interest if the seller agrees to wait. If your money is only a few days late, clause 10.11 says the seller may give you more time but doesn't have to. If they do, you pay interest on the full price at the bank prime rate named in the contract plus 3%, charged daily until they are paid.

Every file is different, and a real estate lawyer is the right person to tell you where you stand legally. Our job is the financing side: making sure the money is really there before you give up the condition.

Why a Pre-Approval Isn't Enough to Waive On

This is where most buyers get caught. A mortgage pre-approval is a good first step. It is not a promise to lend.

The Financial Consumer Agency of Canada says it plainly. A pre-approval is not a final approval, and a lender can still turn you down after pre-approving you. At that stage the lender has usually looked at your credit and the income you told them about. Often nobody has checked your paperwork closely yet, and nobody has looked at the house.

What you want before you waive is a full approval on the actual property. That usually comes as a commitment letter. Even then, read the conditions on it. A commitment often still depends on things like:

  • the appraisal supporting the price
  • your income documents checking out
  • proof of your down payment, usually with about 90 days of history
  • mortgage default insurance approval, if you are putting less than 20% down
  • in some cases, condo documents or proof of home insurance

A commitment with open conditions is stronger than a pre-approval, but it isn't finished. You, your lawyer and your broker should all know what is still open before the waiver is signed.

What Can Still Go Wrong After You Waive

Even with a clean approval, a few things can change between waiving and closing. These are the ones we see most.

The appraisal comes in low. If the appraiser says the home is worth less than you paid, the lender lends on the lower number. You cover the gap in cash. Our guide on what to do when a mortgage appraisal comes in low walks through the options.

The property has a problem. Some homes have issues that make lenders or home insurers nervous, like an old buried oil tank or out-of-date wiring. If the home can't be insured, many lenders won't fund it.

Something changes on your side. Lenders often re-check credit and employment close to the closing date. A new car loan, a job change or a missed payment can undo an approval. We cover this in what not to do before closing on a house.

The down payment isn't where it should be. Money that moves around without a paper trail, or a gift without a proper gift letter, can hold up funding.

Before You Waive Your Financing Condition: A Checklist

  1. Get a full approval on this property, not just a pre-approval. Ask your broker if the file has been fully reviewed by the lender.
  2. Send in every document up front. Job letter, pay stubs, T4s or Notices of Assessment, and 90 days of statements for your down payment.
  3. Ask about the appraisal. Find out if one is needed and whether the price looks supportable for the area.
  4. Read every condition on the commitment letter, and know which ones are still open.
  5. Keep a cash cushion. If you can, hold back money to cover a small appraisal gap.
  6. Don't change anything. No new debt, no job change, no big purchases until you have the keys.
  7. Ask for enough time. In a busy market a short condition period may be all you get. Even a few business days lets a lender look at the actual home.
  8. Talk to your lawyer about the contract before you sign the waiver.

If you are heading into a bidding war, check your numbers with us before you write the offer, not after. That is the one time a same-day answer really matters.

If You Already Waived and the Lender Says No

Don't wait. Call your broker and your lawyer the same day. The more days left before closing, the more options there are.

What is possible depends on why the file was declined, how much you are putting down, and how much time is left. In general terms:

  • Another A-lender. If the problem was one lender's policy, like how it reads your income or the type of property, a different bank, credit union or monoline lender may see it differently.
  • An alternative (B) lender. B-lenders can be more flexible on credit and income. Depending on the whole file, some go below a 600 credit score and allow debt ratios up to roughly 50% GDS and TDS. They usually want more down, often around 20%, and there is typically a lender fee. Our guide to B-lender mortgages explains how they work.
  • A private lender. Private lenders focus on the property and your equity. They generally want 20 to 25% down or more, charge fees, and use short terms. Some can fund within a few days on a rush basis. It costs more, and you need a plan to move to a regular lender later, but it can save a deal and a deposit.
  • A short extension. You can ask the seller for a few more days. Under clause 10.11 of the Alberta form they don't have to agree, and if they do, late interest applies. Your lawyer handles that request.

One hard truth: if you were putting less than 20% down, the B and private options are much harder to reach, because they usually need more down. That is why buyers with small down payments should think hardest before waiving.

None of these options are sure things, and the costs are real. But a year or two with a higher-cost lender is often cheaper than losing a deposit and facing a claim for the price gap.

Frequently Asked Questions

What happens if I waive my financing condition and can't get a mortgage?

The deal is firm, so you are still bound to close. If you can't, you can lose your deposit, and the seller may also claim damages, such as the difference if they resell for less, plus some of their costs. Call your broker and lawyer right away, because another lender may still be able to fund.

Can I get my deposit back if my financing falls through?

If your financing condition is still in place and it isn't met or waived by the Condition Day, the deposit generally comes back to you. Once you have waived the condition, the deposit is usually at risk. Your lawyer can confirm how your own contract reads.

Is a mortgage pre-approval enough to waive my financing condition?

Usually not on its own. A pre-approval is a first look at your credit and income, and it doesn't cover the home you are buying. A lender can still decline you. A full approval on the actual home, with your documents already reviewed, is a much safer place to waive from.

How long is a financing condition on a house?

There is no set length. It is whatever you and the seller agree to in the contract, and in a busy market sellers push for shorter periods. Before you pick a date, ask your broker how long the lender needs to review your documents and the property.

Can a seller sue a buyer for not closing on a house?

Yes. The standard Alberta purchase contract lets a seller keep the deposit and also claim damages and reasonable legal costs. The most common claim is the drop in price if the home resells for less. The seller does have to take reasonable steps to limit that loss.
