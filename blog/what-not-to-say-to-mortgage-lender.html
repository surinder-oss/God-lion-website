What Not to Say to a Mortgage Lender, and What You Must Tell Them

Book a Free Consultation

You're sitting across from a lender, and they ask about your down payment. You almost say, "My brother is lending it to me, but we'll call it a gift." That one sentence can turn a normal file into a fraud problem.

People search for what not to say to a mortgage lender because they're scared one wrong word will sink their approval. The real answer is simpler. The words that hurt you are the ones that aren't true, or the true ones you explain badly. This guide shows the comments that raise flags, what to say instead, and what you must tell a lender even when you'd rather not.

Why Lenders Ask So Many Questions

A lender is about to lend you hundreds of thousands of dollars. Every question on the application ties back to one thing: can you make the payments, and is the home what you say it is?

They check most of your answers anyway. Your credit report shows your debts and any past insolvency. Your bank statements show where your down payment came from. Your pay stubs, job letter or tax returns show your income. If the lender is using mortgage default insurance, the insurer may review the file too.

So the goal isn't to say less. It's to make sure what you say matches what the documents will show, and that anything unusual comes with a clear explanation.

What Not to Say to a Mortgage Lender (and What to Say Instead)

Here are the comments we hear most often that cause trouble. In every case, the fix is the truth, explained clearly.

"I'm about to quit my job" or "I'm starting my own business next month"

If it's true, the lender needs to know. Your approval is based on the income you have now. A job change before closing can change everything, especially if you move into a probation period or switch to self-employment.

What to say instead: tell your broker before you hand in your notice. Many job changes are fine if they're planned well, and some lenders work with new jobs and probation periods. Our guide on getting a mortgage with a new job explains how lenders look at it.

"My brother is lending me the down payment"

This is the big one. A gift and a loan are two different things. A gift letter says the money doesn't have to be paid back. If you sign one for money you do have to repay, that's a misrepresentation.

What to say instead: say exactly what it is. If it's a true gift from family, most lenders want a signed gift letter and proof the money landed in your account before closing. Our gifted down payment guide covers the paperwork. If it's a loan, say so. Some lenders and insurers accept a borrowed down payment in limited cases, as long as it's disclosed and the repayment fits in your debt ratios.

"I'm planning to rent it out"

Lenders treat a home you'll live in and a rental very differently. A rental usually needs at least 20% down, and the rules for counting rental income are different. Telling a lender you'll live in the home when you plan to rent it out is one of the most common forms of mortgage fraud.

What to say instead: describe your real plan. If you'll live in it, say so. If it's an investment, say that, and we'll look at rental programs. Life can change after you move in. What matters is that your answer is true when you apply.

"I get paid some cash on the side"

Most lenders can only use income you can prove, which usually means income you've declared to the CRA. Saying you earn more than your documents show doesn't help, and inflating income is misrepresentation.

What to say instead: give your real, declared income and let us find a lender that fits it. Self-employed borrowers have more than one path. Some banks use a two-year average of declared income or a stated-income program. Some alternative (B) lenders can use about 12 months of business bank statements. Private lenders look mostly at your equity.

"Can we leave that debt off?"

No. Credit cards, car loans, lines of credit, student loans and buy-now-pay-later plans show up on your credit report. Support payments and money you owe the CRA show up in other documents. Leaving a debt off the application doesn't hide it. It just makes your file look careless.

What You Must Tell a Mortgage Lender

Knowing what not to say to a mortgage lender is only half of it. The other half is what you have to say out loud, even when it's uncomfortable. Plan to disclose all of these:

  • Every debt. Including loans you co-signed for someone else. A co-signed loan counts as your debt too.
  • Support payments. Child or spousal support you pay reduces what you can borrow. Support you receive may count as income, but the rules vary by lender.
  • A past bankruptcy or consumer proposal. The application asks, it shows on your credit report for years, and lenders can search the federal insolvency records.
  • Tax owing to the CRA. Lenders often ask for your Notice of Assessment, which shows any balance.
  • Where your down payment comes from. Savings, a gift, the sale of another property, or a loan. Most lenders want about three months of statements showing the money.
  • Job or income changes. Before you apply and any time before closing.
  • Other properties you own. Their mortgages, taxes and any rent they bring in.
  • Who will live in the home. You, family, or tenants.

A lot of people worry that disclosing a past problem means an automatic no. It usually doesn't. A bank may want a consumer proposal paid off and some rebuilt credit first. Some B-lenders can work with a proposal that's still active, can go below a 600 credit score depending on the overall file, and allow debt ratios up to roughly 50/50. Private lenders focus on equity and usually want 20% to 25% or more down. If you've been through a proposal, our mortgage after a consumer proposal guide lays out the options.

What Happens If You Don't Disclose Something

CMHC describes mortgage fraud as deliberately giving false information to get a mortgage you wouldn't have been approved for if the lender knew the truth. Its mortgage fraud guidance lists inflating income, misstating your job, misrepresenting the source of your down payment, and calling a rental owner-occupied.

The consequences are serious and can show up long after closing:

  • The lender can treat it as a breach of your mortgage contract and ask for the full balance back.
  • Mortgage default insurance can be voided.
  • You stay responsible for any shortfall if the home is sold after a default.
  • You can face criminal charges.

A broker also can't send a file they know is false. If we find out something was hidden, we have to stop. That's why it's always better to tell us early. Most problems have a fix when they're on the table. Very few do once they've been hidden.

Signs Your Mortgage Will Be Approved

People also ask how to tell if a file is on track. A pre-approval is not a final approval, but these are good signs:

  1. Your documents were reviewed up front. A pre-approval where someone actually checked your pay stubs, statements and credit is much stronger than one based on a quick conversation.
  2. The lender's requests are specific. "Send the March statement for account ending 123" is a normal condition. Broad, repeated questions about the same item can mean something doesn't add up.
  3. The appraisal came in at or near the price.
  4. You have a written commitment with a clear list of conditions, and you're checking them off one by one.
  5. Nothing has changed since you applied. Same job, same debts, no new credit.

If you've already been declined somewhere, it helps to know why. Our post on what disqualifies you from a mortgage breaks down which problems stop you everywhere and which only stop you at a bank.

Frequently Asked Questions

What should you not say to a mortgage lender?

Don't say anything that isn't true, like calling a loan a gift, saying you'll live in a home you plan to rent out, or inflating your income. Also avoid vague comments about quitting your job or "leaving a debt off." Give the real facts and let your broker explain anything unusual.

Do I have to tell my mortgage lender about a consumer proposal?

Yes. Applications ask about past bankruptcies and consumer proposals, and a proposal shows on your credit report for years. Disclosing it doesn't mean an automatic no. Some lenders work with a proposal that's paid off or even still active, depending on the rest of your file.

Is it mortgage fraud to say a loan is a gift?

Yes, it can be. A gift letter states the money doesn't have to be repaid. If it does, that's misrepresenting the source of your down payment. If your down payment is borrowed, say so. Some lenders accept a borrowed down payment in limited cases when it's disclosed.

Can I tell my lender I'll live in the home and rent it out later?

You must answer based on your real plan when you apply. If you truly plan to live there, it's owner-occupied, even if life changes later. If you plan to rent it out from the start, tell the lender. A rental usually needs at least 20% down.

What are signs your mortgage will be approved?

Good signs include a pre-approval where your documents were actually reviewed, specific condition requests from the lender, an appraisal at or near the price, and a written commitment you're working through. Keeping your job, debts and credit the same until closing matters too.
