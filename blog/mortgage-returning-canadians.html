Moving Back to Canada? How a Mortgage for Returning Canadians Works

Book a Free Consultation

Pick a time that works for you — no obligation

You grew up here. You have a Canadian passport. Then you spent five or ten years working in Dubai, London or Sydney, and now you're coming home. You call a bank about a mortgage, and they treat you like you just arrived for the first time.

That surprises a lot of people. A mortgage for returning Canadians has its own set of hurdles: a credit file that went quiet, income earned in another country, and savings sitting in a foreign bank. None of these are deal breakers. But each one needs a plan, and the order you do things in matters. This guide walks through what lenders look at and how to set yourself up before and after you land.

Why Coming Home Can Feel Like Starting Over

Being a citizen settles one big question. The federal ban on foreign buyers doesn't apply to Canadian citizens or permanent residents, even if you live abroad. You're allowed to buy.

Being allowed to buy and getting a mortgage are two different things, though. A lender doesn't care much about your passport. It cares about three things it can check: your credit, your income, and where your down payment came from. After years away, all three can look thin on paper.

  • Credit: your Canadian credit file may show little or no recent activity.
  • Income: most lenders want income they can verify in Canada, and yours may be from a foreign employer.
  • Down payment: money moved from overseas needs a clear paper trail.

The good news is that lenders see returning Canadians all the time, and a mortgage for returning Canadians is a normal request for a broker. Some banks fold returning citizens into the same programs they use for newcomers, and those programs were built for exactly this kind of file.

Buying Before You Move Back vs After You Land

This is the first decision, and it changes almost everything else.

Buying while you still live abroad. A lender will usually treat you as a non-resident, even as a citizen. Non-resident mortgages often need a much larger down payment, commonly around 35% or more, and fewer lenders offer them. You'll also need the down payment already in Canada and well documented. It can be done, and it makes sense for some people, but it's the harder road.

Buying after you've moved back and started work here. Once you have a Canadian job and income, your file starts to look like any other borrower's. That opens up insured mortgages, where the minimum down payment is 5% of the first $500,000 and 10% of the portion above that, up to $1.5 million (as of 2026). Not everyone will fit, but the options get much wider.

A middle path many people take: sign a job offer in Canada, move back, start work, then buy. Some lenders will look at a signed offer before your first day, though probation periods and start dates can affect that. We cover this in more detail in our guide to getting a mortgage with a new job. Renting for a few months first also gives you time to rebuild credit and get your paperwork in order.

Your Canadian Credit After Years Away

Your credit history from another country generally doesn't move with you. So the question is what's left in your Canadian file.

If you kept a Canadian credit card open and used it now and then while you were away, you may be in good shape. If you closed everything when you left, your file may show nothing recent. Lenders read that the same way they read a thin file for someone who never had credit. It's not bad credit. It's just not enough to judge.

Here's what helps:

  1. Pull your reports first. Check both Equifax and TransUnion before you apply for anything. Look for old accounts, wrong addresses, or anything you don't recognize.
  2. Open a secured or regular credit card as soon as you land. Use it for small bills and pay it in full every month.
  3. Gather proof of good credit abroad. Some lenders will accept a reference letter from your foreign bank, on letterhead, confirming your accounts were handled well. A strong rental history helps too.
  4. Don't apply for lots of credit at once. A few months of clean history beats a pile of new accounts.

If your score comes back lower than you hoped, it's worth knowing that the cutoff depends on the lender type. Our post on the minimum credit score for a mortgage explains how A-lenders, B-lenders and private lenders each look at it.

Income: What Counts When You've Been Working Abroad

Income is where most returning Canadians hit the wall, so it pays to understand the full range of lenders.

A-lenders (the big banks and most credit unions) usually want Canadian income they can verify, like pay stubs and an employment letter from a Canadian employer. Many won't use foreign employment income at all. Some programs also ask for a short stretch of full-time work in Canada first, often a few months, though people moved here by their employer through a corporate transfer are sometimes treated differently.

B-lenders (alternative lenders) can be more flexible. They may look harder at your whole picture: your savings, your down payment, and the strength of your new job, even if your Canadian work history is short. Expect a bigger down payment and a higher cost than an A-lender.

Private lenders focus mostly on the property and your equity. They usually want 20% to 25% down or more, and they're best used as a short bridge while you build a file that an A-lender will accept.

A few special cases come up often:

  • Working remotely in Canada for a foreign company. Some lenders will consider it, especially once you're filing taxes here. Many won't. This one is very lender-specific.
  • Foreign pensions or investment income. Some lenders will count steady foreign income with proof that it continues. Others won't.
  • Self-employed and coming home. You'll usually need a track record of Canadian business income, or a lender that can work from bank statements.

Our guide to foreign income mortgages in Canada goes deeper on which documents lenders accept as proof of income from abroad.

Down Payment From Overseas: Rules for a Mortgage for Returning Canadians

Lenders need to see where your down payment came from. That's true for everyone, but money from abroad gets a closer look.

  • Move it early. Most lenders want to see about 90 days of history on your down payment. Moving the money into a Canadian account well before you buy makes that easy.
  • Keep the trail clean. Save the statements from your foreign account showing the money building up, plus the wire or transfer receipts. Statements not in English or French may need a certified translation.
  • Avoid many small transfers. One or two clear transfers are much easier to document than ten small ones.
  • Gifts are possible, with limits. Many lenders accept a gift from immediate family with a signed gift letter and proof the money arrived. Non-resident programs are often stricter about gifts. Our gifted down payment guide covers the paperwork.
  • Plan for closing costs too. Lenders usually want to see about 1.5% of the price set aside for legal fees and other costs on top of the down payment.

First-Time Buyer Programs When You Come Home

Many returning Canadians have never owned a home in Canada, so they ask about first-time buyer programs. Here's the short version.

The First Home Savings Account (FHSA) and the RRSP Home Buyers' Plan both require you to be a resident of Canada. So they generally help after you've moved back, not while you live abroad. Both also use a first-time buyer test. It looks back about four years and asks if you lived in a home you owned.

One thing to watch: the government's wording doesn't limit that test to homes in Canada. If you owned and lived in a home overseas in the last few years, check with an accountant before you count on these programs. This is also a good time to get tax advice on becoming a resident again in general.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a Canadian citizen living abroad get a mortgage in Canada?

Yes, but most lenders will treat you as a non-resident while you live outside Canada. That often means a larger down payment, commonly around 35% or more, and fewer lenders to choose from. Once you move back and earn Canadian income, many more options open up.

Do returning Canadians need Canadian credit history to get a mortgage?

It helps a lot, but it isn't always required. Some lenders accept other proof, such as a reference letter from your foreign bank or a strong rental history. Opening a Canadian credit card as soon as you land and paying it in full builds your file quickly.

Does foreign income count for a mortgage in Canada?

It depends on the lender. Most big banks want Canadian income and won't use foreign employment income. Some lenders will count steady foreign pension or investment income with proof that it continues, and alternative lenders can be more flexible.

How much down payment does a returning Canadian need?

If you've moved back and have Canadian income, you may qualify for an insured mortgage with as little as 5% down on the first $500,000 and 10% on the portion above that, up to $1.5 million. If you buy while still living abroad, expect around 35% or more. Your exact number depends on your file.

Can I use the FHSA if I'm moving back to Canada?

You need to be a resident of Canada to open an FHSA, so it usually helps after you've moved back. You also need to pass the first-time buyer test, which asks if you lived in a home you owned in about the last four years. Check with an accountant if you owned a home abroad.
