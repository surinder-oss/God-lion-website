Thinking About Co-Signing a Mortgage for Your Child? Read This First

Your daughter has a good job, some savings and a pre-approval that's about $150,000 short of the homes she's looking at. Then the lender says, "Do you have a parent who could co-sign?" Now it's your name, your credit and your own borrowing on the line.

Co-signing a mortgage in Canada can open the door for your child. It can also tie up your finances for years if you go in without a plan. Here's what co-signing really means, how lenders look at it, what it does to your own borrowing, and how you get your name off later.

Is It Possible to Co-Sign a Mortgage for Your Child in Canada?

Yes. Co-signing is common and it's getting more common. A Bank of Canada analysis published in April 2026 found that the share of first-time buyer mortgages co-signed by a parent rose from about 4% in 2004 to about 11% in 2025. In the same study, most of the co-signed buyers would not have qualified on their own.

Banks, credit unions, alternative lenders and private lenders can all accept a second person on the file. Mortgage insurers also allow it on high-ratio mortgages (less than 20% down). In fact, the insurer rules say that at least one borrower or guarantor needs a credit score of 600 or more, so a parent's credit can be the piece that makes an insured file work.

What changes from lender to lender is how you're allowed to help. Some lenders only accept a spouse as a guarantor. Some accept immediate family. Some programs don't allow a person who won't live in the home at all. That's why the first question isn't "can I co-sign?" It's "which lender will count my help the way we need?"

Co-Signer vs Guarantor: What's the Difference?

People use "co-signer" as a catch-all word. Lenders usually split it into two roles, and they work differently.

  • Co-borrower (co-owner). You go on the mortgage and on title. You own part of the home and you're fully responsible for the whole mortgage, not just your share. Your income and debts are added to the application.
  • Guarantor. You sign a promise to pay if your child doesn't, but you're not on title. You don't own any part of the home. Some lenders use a guarantor mainly to add credit strength. Others will also count the guarantor's income, depending on the lender and the program.

Can you co-sign without being on title? Yes, that's what a guarantor is. But not every lender offers it for a parent, and the rules on whose income counts are different everywhere. Many lenders also require a guarantor to get independent legal advice from their own lawyer before signing, so you clearly understand what you're agreeing to.

There's a tax side to this too. If you go on title as a co-owner and the home isn't your own principal residence, your share could be exposed to capital gains tax when it's sold. Talk to an accountant before you pick a structure. We can tell you how lenders see each option, but we don't give tax or legal advice.

What Are the Requirements to Be a Co-Signer?

Lenders check a co-signer almost the same way they check the main borrower. Expect them to look at:

  • Your credit. A clean history and a solid score. If your credit has problems, you may not help the file much.
  • Your income. Pay stubs, a job letter, Notices of Assessment, or pension statements if you're retired.
  • Your debts. Your own mortgage, car loans, lines of credit and cards. These all go into the math.
  • Your relationship to the buyer. Many lenders want a parent, a spouse or other close family.
  • ID and legal paperwork, plus independent legal advice where the lender asks for it.

Retired parents can co-sign too. Lenders count CPP, OAS, pensions and steady RRIF income. Our guide on getting a mortgage when you're retired explains how each type of retirement income is usually treated.

How Much Does a Co-Signer Help a Mortgage?

It depends on what's holding your child back. A co-signer usually helps in one of three ways:

  1. Not enough income. If the lender counts your income, it can raise how much your child qualifies for. In the Bank of Canada study, a parent's co-signature raised the typical buyer's maximum price by roughly 70%.
  2. Thin or bruised credit. A young buyer with only a year of credit, or a buyer rebuilding after a rough patch, can get over the line with a parent's strong credit behind them. Our post on joint mortgages with bad credit shows how lenders read two very different scores.
  3. Short job history. A new grad or someone on probation may look risky on paper. A co-signer can calm that worry for some lenders.

A co-signer can't fix everything. The down payment still has to meet the minimum, the property still has to qualify, and the stress test still applies on most new mortgages. And if the plan only works because the payment is stretched to the limit, that's a warning sign. The same Bank of Canada study found that buyers who used the most of their extra borrowing room later saw the biggest rise in missed payments on cards and lines of credit.

Is Co-Signing a Mortgage a Good Idea? What It Does to Your Own Borrowing

This is the part most parents don't hear until it's too late. When you co-sign, that mortgage becomes your debt too. When you apply for your own mortgage, renewal switch, refinance or line of credit later, lenders will usually count some or all of your child's mortgage against you.

Here's a simple example. Say your child's payment is $2,600 a month. When you go to refinance your own home, many lenders will add that $2,600 to your monthly debts. Some lenders will reduce or remove it if your child can show 12 months or more of on-time payments from their own account. Others won't, especially if you're on title. Rules vary a lot, so this needs to be checked before you sign.

A few more things to think about:

  • Late payments hit your credit too. If your child misses a payment, it can show up on your credit report.
  • Insured mortgage limits. Mortgage insurers limit how many insured homes one person can be on at the same time. If you already have an insured mortgage, or plan to buy a rental, check this first.
  • Relationships change. If your child later separates from a partner who's also on title, your name is still on the mortgage while they sort it out.

Co-signing is a good idea when your child can afford the payment on their own today, and just needs help getting approved. It's risky when your income is what really makes the payment possible.

How to Remove a Co-Signer From a Mortgage Later

Plan your exit on day one. Coming off a mortgage almost always needs the lender's approval, and your child has to qualify on their own at that point. The usual ways out are:

  • At renewal. When the term ends, your child applies on their own. If their income has grown, this is often the cleanest time.
  • A refinance. Your child refinances into their own name. If you're on title, title is updated at the same time. Expect legal fees and, if it's mid-term, a possible prepayment penalty.
  • A switch to another lender at renewal, again in your child's name only.

Write down a target date, like "at the first renewal," and look at the numbers a few months before it. If your child isn't there yet, an alternative lender for one term can sometimes be a bridge. B-lenders can work with credit scores below 600 and debt ratios up to roughly 50%, depending on the overall file, though rates and fees are higher.

Other Ways to Help Instead of Co-Signing

Co-signing isn't the only way to help. Depending on your finances and theirs, one of these may fit better:

  • Gift part of the down payment. A bigger down payment shrinks the mortgage, and a gift doesn't stay on your credit. Lenders need a signed gift letter saying it doesn't have to be paid back. Our guide to gifted down payments covers the paperwork.
  • Buy together on purpose. If you want to own part of the home, set it up as a planned family purchase with a written agreement. Our post on intergenerational mortgages in Alberta walks through the main setups.
  • Help clear their debts first. Paying off a car loan or credit card can raise what your child qualifies for more than you'd expect, with no long-term tie to you.

If your child is buying their first home, our first-time buyer mortgage page explains the programs and steps that can lower how much help they need.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a parent co-sign a mortgage in Canada?

Yes. Many lenders accept a parent as a co-borrower or a guarantor, and mortgage insurers allow it on high-ratio mortgages too. Each lender has its own rules on who can co-sign and whether the co-signer's income counts.

What is the difference between a co-signer and a guarantor?

A co-borrower goes on the mortgage and on title, so they own part of the home. A guarantor signs a promise to pay if the borrower doesn't, but isn't on title. Both are fully responsible for the debt if payments stop.

Does co-signing a mortgage affect my ability to get my own mortgage?

Usually, yes. Most lenders count some or all of the co-signed mortgage payment as your debt. Some will reduce it if the main borrower shows 12 months or more of on-time payments, but rules vary by lender.

How do I get my name off a mortgage I co-signed?

The lender has to approve it, and the main borrower must qualify on their own. This usually happens at renewal, through a refinance, or by switching lenders in the borrower's name only.

Can retired parents co-sign a mortgage?

Yes. Lenders can count CPP, OAS, pension and steady RRIF income for a retired co-signer. Your own debts and credit still matter, and the full payment may count against you later.
