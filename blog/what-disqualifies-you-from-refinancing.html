What Disqualifies You From Refinancing Your Mortgage, and What Can You Do About It?

You've paid your mortgage on time for years. Your home is worth more than when you bought it. So when you ask your bank to refinance and the answer is no, it stings. It feels like you did everything right.

Here's what most people don't know. A refinance is a brand-new mortgage application. The lender checks everything again, as if you'd never met. So what disqualifies you from refinancing usually isn't one big thing. It's one of six checks that didn't line up. This guide walks through each one in plain words, and what you can do if you hit a wall.

A Refinance Is a New Mortgage, Not a Renewal

At renewal, you keep the same balance and sign up for a new term. Most lenders don't make you qualify again. Since late 2024, you can also switch to a new lender at renewal without the stress test, as long as you don't borrow more or stretch out your amortization.

A refinance is different. You're changing the mortgage itself. That could mean taking money out, paying off debts, adding or removing someone from title, or resetting your amortization. Because the loan changes, the lender looks at your income, your credit, your home and your title all over again.

That's why someone with a perfect payment record can still be turned down. The old approval doesn't carry over. You have to pass today's rules with today's numbers.

1. Not Enough Equity: the 80% Limit

This is the most common reason. In Canada, you can usually borrow up to 80% of your home's appraised value on a refinance. That 80% covers everything secured against the home: your first mortgage, any HELOC and any second mortgage, all added together. The Financial Consumer Agency of Canada explains the same limit for borrowing against home equity.

Here's a simple example with round numbers:

  • Your home appraises at $500,000.
  • 80% of that is $400,000. That's the most you can owe after the refinance.
  • If you owe $380,000 today, you only have about $20,000 to work with, before any penalty or fees.

A few things shrink your room even more. The appraisal may come in lower than you hoped. If you bought the home recently, some lenders will use your purchase price instead of a new appraisal. And most refinances can't be covered by mortgage default insurance, so you can't go above 80% the way a buyer with a small down payment can.

There is one exception worth knowing. Since January 15, 2025, federal rules allow an insured refinance of up to 90% of the home's value to build a secondary suite, counting the value the suite adds. Conditions apply, and not every lender offers it, so ask before you plan around it.

2. Your Income Doesn't Pass the Stress Test

Even with plenty of equity, you have to show you can afford the new mortgage. As of 2026, federally regulated lenders qualify uninsured mortgages at the higher of your contract rate plus 2% or 5.25%. That includes a refinance with your own bank.

Lenders then look at two debt ratios. Most banks want your housing costs at about 39% or less of your gross income, and all your debt payments at about 44% or less. A bigger loan means a bigger payment, so a refinance can push you over the line even if your current mortgage fits fine.

One piece of good news: if you're refinancing to pay off credit cards or a car loan, the lender usually counts those debts as paid. That can help your ratios a lot.

Changes in your work life also matter. A new job still on probation, fewer hours, being on parental leave, or switching to self-employment can all stop a refinance at a bank. Self-employed income has more than one path, though:

  • Banks and credit unions usually use a two-year average from your Notices of Assessment, or a stated-income program for business owners.
  • Alternative (B) lenders can often use about 12 months of business bank statements and may not need your most recent NOA.
  • Private lenders focus mostly on your equity and your plan to pay them back.

If you want the full list of things that stop any mortgage, not just a refinance, see our guide on what disqualifies you from a mortgage.

3. Credit Problems and Missed Payments

Your credit gets pulled again on a refinance. These are the issues that most often cause a no:

  • Late payments on your current mortgage. This is the biggest red flag. A lender wants to see that the loan on this very home has been paid on time.
  • Collections, maxed-out cards, or a low score. Banks have score minimums, and a few recent late payments can drop you below them.
  • A consumer proposal or bankruptcy. If it's still active or only recently finished, most banks will say no.
  • Property tax arrears or money owed to CRA. Unpaid property taxes and CRA debts can rank ahead of a mortgage, so lenders want them cleared as part of the refinance.

A bank's no on credit is not the end of the road. Alternative lenders look at the whole file and can go below a 600 credit score, depending on your equity, income and the reason behind the credit trouble. Many will approve a refinance that pays off the proposal, the tax arrears or the collections in one step.

4. Title and Ownership Problems

A refinance has to be signed off by everyone on title. That causes trouble more often than people expect.

  • A co-owner won't sign. If you're separated and your ex is still on title, you'll usually need a signed separation agreement before a lender will refinance to buy them out.
  • Liens or judgments. A builder's lien, a court judgment or a CRA lien registered on title has to be dealt with first.
  • Dower consent in Alberta. If your spouse lives in the home but isn't on title, they'll usually need to sign a Dower consent so the lender can register the new mortgage.

Adding or removing a person from title also counts as a refinance, so the person staying on title has to qualify on their own income.

5. The Property Itself

The lender is lending against your home, so the home has to fit their rules too. These can stop a refinance at a bank:

  • The home needs big repairs, like a roof, foundation or water damage.
  • It's an unusual property, such as a large acreage, a mobile home on leased land, or a home with a business on the main floor.
  • Rent from a suite that isn't legal or permitted. Many lenders won't count that income.
  • A condo with a big special assessment or a weak reserve fund.
  • A rental property. Some lenders allow less equity to be taken out of a rental than out of a home you live in.

6. Your Current Mortgage Gets in the Way

Sometimes you qualify, but the numbers don't make sense. Breaking a fixed mortgage mid-term usually costs three months' interest or an interest rate differential, whichever is higher. On a big balance, that can wipe out the savings. Our guide to the cost to refinance a mortgage in Alberta walks through the penalty and a simple break-even test.

Some mortgages are also registered as a collateral charge. That doesn't stop a refinance, but moving it to a new lender can mean extra legal fees. If your term ends in a few months, waiting for renewal is often the cheaper move.

What Disqualifies You From Refinancing at a Bank May Not Stop Every Lender

A bank has one shelf of products. When you don't fit it, they say no. But there's usually more than one way to get to your goal. Depending on your file, these are the options we look at:

  • Wait for renewal and refinance then, with no penalty.
  • Ask your current lender for extra funds or a blended rate, if you still qualify with them.
  • A HELOC or a second mortgage that leaves your first mortgage alone. Our post on HELOC vs refinance vs second mortgage compares the three.
  • An alternative (B) lender refinance. These lenders can often go up to about 80% of value, accept lower scores, and allow debt ratios up to roughly 50/50. The rate and fees are higher, so it's usually a one- to two-year bridge. Learn how it works in our B-lender mortgage guide.
  • A private mortgage for short-term needs. Private lenders focus on equity, cost more, and need a clear exit plan.
  • Fix what you can first. Pay down a card, dispute an error on your credit report, or add a co-signer, then apply again.

Frequently Asked Questions

What disqualifies you from refinancing a mortgage in Canada?

The most common reasons are not enough equity, income that doesn't pass the stress test, recent missed payments or other credit problems, title issues, and a property the lender won't accept. A refinance is a new application, so you have to qualify again even if you've never missed a payment.

How much equity do I need to refinance my mortgage?

You can usually borrow up to 80% of your home's appraised value, including all mortgages and HELOCs on the home. So you need more than 20% equity to take any money out. The exception is an insured refinance to build a secondary suite, which can go up to 90% under federal rules.

Can I refinance my mortgage with bad credit?

Often, yes, but maybe not with a bank. Alternative lenders can approve scores below 600 depending on your equity and income, and private lenders focus mostly on equity. The rate is higher, so many people use it for a year or two while they rebuild their credit.

Do I have to pass the stress test to refinance with my own bank?

Yes, if your bank is federally regulated and you're borrowing more or changing the loan. As of 2026, you qualify at the higher of your contract rate plus 2% or 5.25%. A straight renewal or switch, with no extra money and no longer amortization, doesn't need the stress test.

Can I refinance if I'm behind on property taxes?

It depends on your equity and the lender. Many banks will decline while taxes are in arrears. Alternative and private lenders will often approve a refinance that pays the taxes off at closing, as long as there's enough equity to cover them.
