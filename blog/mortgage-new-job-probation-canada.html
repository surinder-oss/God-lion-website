Just Started a New Job? How a Mortgage With a New Job Really Works

You just landed a better job. The pay is higher, the role is steady, and now you want to buy. Then a friend tells you nobody will give you a mortgage with a new job until your probation is over. That's partly true and partly a myth, and the difference matters if you've found a home you like.

The short answer: many people do get a mortgage with a new job, sometimes even while on probation. It depends on how you're paid, whether you stayed in the same line of work, and which lender looks at your file. Here's how lenders actually think about it.

Why Lenders Care About a New Job

A lender is lending you money for 25 or 30 years. What they want to know is simple: will this income keep coming in?

A new job raises two questions. First, can the employer let you go easily? In Alberta, an employer generally doesn't have to give notice of termination in the first 90 days of a job, and many employers set a probation period of three to six months. Second, is this income new to you, or is it the same kind of work you've done for years?

The second question usually matters more than the first. Someone who has been a nurse for eight years and just moved to a new hospital is a very different file from someone who just switched careers. Both have a new job. Only one has a long track record in the work.

Can You Get a Mortgage With a New Job While on Probation?

Often, yes, but not everywhere. Here's how the main lender types tend to look at it. Every lender has its own policy, so treat these as general patterns, not rules.

  • Banks and credit unions (A-lenders): many ask for written confirmation that you're not on probation, especially if you've been in the role for six months or less. Some will still consider a probationary file if the rest is strong, but it usually needs extra review. Many also look for about two years of work history, and time in school often counts toward that.
  • Pay-history programs: some lenders have programs that look at your regular pay deposits into your bank account, instead of relying only on a job letter that confirms you're permanent. These can help when an employer won't write a letter until probation ends.
  • Alternative lenders (B-lenders): these lenders are often more flexible on job tenure and probation. They'll usually want a bigger down payment, often 20% or more, and the rate and fees are higher. In return, they can sometimes go up to roughly 50% / 50% on debt ratios (GDS/TDS) and may work with credit scores below 600, depending on the whole file.
  • Private lenders: these focus mostly on your equity and down payment, usually 20% to 25% or more. They're a short-term bridge, not a long-term home for your mortgage.

If you're buying with less than 20% down, your mortgage will also need default insurance. That means the lender and the insurer both need to be comfortable with your job. A strong file in every other way, with good credit and low debt, makes that much easier.

How You're Paid Changes Everything

The single biggest factor in getting a mortgage with a new job is how your pay is set up. Two people can start new jobs on the same day and get very different answers.

  • Salaried, guaranteed pay: this is the easiest case. If you're in the same field and the salary is on your job letter, many lenders can use it right away, once probation is cleared or confirmed.
  • Hourly with guaranteed hours: usually workable. Your job letter should state your hourly rate and your minimum guaranteed hours per week.
  • Hourly without guaranteed hours, or part-time: harder. Many lenders want a history of the actual hours you've worked, often averaged over time, before they'll count it.
  • Overtime, bonus and commission: this is where a new job hurts the most. Lenders usually average these over two years, and many want that history with your current employer. In a brand-new job, they may count only your base pay, or nothing for the variable part. Our guide to getting a mortgage on commission income explains how that averaging works.
  • Switching to self-employment: going from a paycheque to your own business resets the clock at most banks. They usually want two years of tax returns from the business. Some alternative lenders can look at about 12 months of business bank statements instead. Our self-employed mortgage page covers those paths.

So a salaried engineer who moved companies at a higher salary is in a very different spot than a salesperson who left a salaried job for a commission-only role. Same "new job," completely different mortgage.

Same Field, Better Pay: The Strongest New-Job File

Lenders love continuity. The best version of a new-job file looks like this:

  • You stayed in the same line of work, or moved up within it.
  • Your pay went up or stayed the same.
  • Your pay is salaried or guaranteed hourly.
  • You have no gaps in work, or a short gap you can explain.
  • You've finished probation, or your employer confirms you're not on one.

If your file looks like that, you may not need to wait at all. If it only ticks two or three of those boxes, the right lender matters a lot more.

People who are new to Canada face an extra layer here, because lenders also look at your permit and how long you've worked in Canada. Our guide to buying a home on a work permit covers that side.

Documents That Help When Your Job Is New

A new-job file lives or dies on paperwork. Get these ready before you apply:

  • A job letter on company letterhead, signed and recent. It should show your start date, position, salary or hourly rate, guaranteed hours, and whether you're on probation. If you are, ask for the probation end date and a line that says you're expected to be made permanent.
  • Your signed offer letter or employment contract.
  • Your first pay stub, or your most recent one if you've been there a few weeks.
  • Proof of your past work, like last year's T4 or Notice of Assessment, and your previous job title. This shows the lender you've done this work before.
  • Bank statements showing your pay landing in your account.

One small detail catches a lot of people: if your job letter doesn't mention probation at all, some lenders read that as non-probationary. Don't try to hide anything, though. Lenders often call your employer to confirm, and an honest file is always easier to place than one that falls apart in underwriting.

Should You Wait Until Probation Ends?

Sometimes waiting is the smart move. Sometimes it costs you the house. Here's how we usually think about it with clients.

Waiting may make sense if: you switched careers, your new pay is mostly commission or bonus, your probation ends in a few weeks anyway, or your down payment is small and you need the widest choice of lenders.

Moving now may make sense if: you're in the same field at the same or better pay, your employer will confirm your status in writing, and you've found the right home. A lender who reads your file well can make waiting unnecessary.

Getting pre-approved is also smart. It tells you what's likely before you write an offer. Our Calgary mortgage pre-approval guide explains what a pre-approval does and doesn't promise.

If you change jobs after you're approved but before you close, that's a different problem. Tell your broker before you accept the new offer. Our post on what not to do before closing on a house explains why a job change mid-deal can stop a file.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I get a mortgage if I just started a new job?

Often, yes. Many lenders will use your income right away if you're salaried or have guaranteed hours and stayed in the same line of work. Commission, bonus and overtime are harder, because lenders usually want a two-year history before they count them.

Can you get a mortgage while on probation in Canada?

Sometimes. Many banks prefer you to be past probation, but some will consider a probationary file if the rest of it is strong. Alternative lenders are often more flexible, usually with a larger down payment. A broker can tell you which lenders fit your situation.

How long do you need to be at a job to get a mortgage?

There's no single rule. Many lenders look for about two years of work history in total, not two years at the same employer. If you're in the same field at the same or higher pay, a few weeks at a new job can be enough with the right lender.

Does changing jobs affect my mortgage pre-approval?

It can. A pre-approval is based on the job you had when you applied. If you change jobs, the lender has to review your new income. Tell your broker before you accept a new offer, not after.

What should my job letter say for a mortgage?

It should be on letterhead, signed, and recent. Include your start date, position, salary or hourly rate, guaranteed hours, and whether you're on probation. If you are, ask your employer to add the end date and that you're expected to become permanent.
