What Happens to the Mortgage When a Spouse Dies: A Plain Guide

Book a Free Consultation

Pick a time that works for you — no obligation

When a spouse dies, the last thing anyone wants to think about is the mortgage. But the payment still comes out next month, and a lot of people lie awake wondering one thing: will I lose the house?

In most cases, no. The mortgage when a spouse dies doesn't disappear, but it doesn't get called due overnight either. What happens next depends on three things: whose name is on title, whose name is on the mortgage, and whether there's insurance. This guide walks through each one in plain language, so you know what to do first and what can wait.

First: Who Owns the Home Now?

The mortgage and the ownership of the home are two separate things. Ownership is about title, the record at the land titles office that says who owns the property. How you and your spouse held title makes a big difference.

  • Joint tenancy. This is how most couples hold title. When one owner dies, the other owns the whole home automatically. It doesn't go through the will or the estate. In Alberta, you update the title by sending the land titles office proof of death, such as a death certificate, along with the right form. Probate isn't needed for this step.
  • Tenants in common. Each owner holds a share, often 50/50. When one owner dies, their share goes to their estate and is handled under their will. The surviving spouse may inherit it, or it may go to someone else, like children from a first marriage.
  • Only your spouse was on title. The home goes into their estate. What happens to it depends on the will, or on provincial law if there's no will.

The Government of Alberta has a page on changing land title ownership, including after a death. If the home goes through an estate, or you're not sure how title is held, talk to a lawyer. We can explain how lenders see each setup, but we don't give legal or estate advice.

What Happens to Your Mortgage When a Spouse Dies?

The mortgage is a debt, and the debt stays until it's paid. Who is responsible for it depends on whose name is on the mortgage itself.

If you were both on the mortgage, you're now fully responsible for the whole balance. That was true the day you signed. The good news is that the terms don't change. Your rate, your payment and your renewal date stay the same as long as the payments keep coming. In most cases the lender won't ask you to pay it off or requalify right away.

If only your spouse was on the mortgage, the debt belongs to their estate. The executor deals with the lender, and the mortgage usually has to be paid from the estate, taken over by the person inheriting the home, or paid off when the home is sold. If you want to keep the home, you'll likely need to apply to put the mortgage in your own name. Lenders will look at your income and credit at that point.

Either way, call the lender early. Tell them what happened and ask what they need. Most lenders have a team that handles a borrower's death, and many will give the family time to sort things out as long as payments are kept up. Some lenders also offer short-term help, like adjusting the payment, if money is tight while the estate is settled. Ask. You don't have to make big decisions in the first few weeks.

Will Insurance Pay Off the Mortgage?

This is where a lot of families get surprised. There are three kinds of "mortgage insurance," and they do very different things.

  • Mortgage default insurance. If you bought with less than 20% down, you paid for this. It protects the lender if the mortgage isn't paid. It does not pay off your mortgage when someone dies.
  • Creditor insurance (mortgage life insurance from the lender). This is the optional coverage many people add when they sign. If the person who died was covered and the claim is approved, it pays the remaining balance straight to the lender. Check your mortgage papers or call the lender to see if it was in place. Some creditor policies look closely at health history when a claim is made, so the claim isn't always automatic.
  • Personal life insurance. A term or permanent policy pays a lump sum to the beneficiary, not the lender. The survivor can use it to pay off the mortgage, pay it down, or keep it as a cushion. It's often the most flexible option.

There's also a small government payment. The Canada Pension Plan pays a one-time death benefit of up to $2,500 to the estate or eligible family members, if your spouse paid into CPP. A surviving spouse may also qualify for a monthly CPP survivor's pension. It won't cover the mortgage, but it's worth applying for.

Can You Keep the House on One Income?

This is the big question for most people. If the payments were built around two incomes, you may now be looking at the same payment on one.

Start with the numbers. Add up your income, any survivor's pension, any employer pension that carries on to a spouse, and any insurance money. Then look at what the home really costs each month: mortgage, property tax, heat and condo fees. If the life insurance pays the mortgage down, your payment may drop a lot.

If you're both on the mortgage, you usually don't have to requalify until you want to change something. Here's when your income gets looked at again:

  • At renewal with the same lender. Many lenders will let you take your spouse's name off with a death certificate and carry on. Some treat it as a new application. Ask early, a few months before your renewal date. Our mortgage renewal page explains how the process works.
  • Switching lenders at renewal. Since late 2024, a straight switch at renewal (same balance and amortization) no longer needs the stress test at most federally regulated lenders. That can make moving easier on one income. Here's more on switching lenders at renewal without the stress test.
  • Refinancing. If you need to take equity out, change the amortization, or buy out a share held by the estate, the lender will qualify you on your own income.

The math of qualifying on one income is a lot like what separated couples go through. Our guide on qualifying for a mortgage on one income shows how lenders count each income source and how a longer amortization can lower the payment.

Your Options if the Payment Is Too High

If the numbers don't work as they are, you have more choices than most people think. The right one depends on your age, your equity, your income and how you feel about the home.

  • Stay with a regular lender (an A-lender). Banks and credit unions usually want your total debts to stay within roughly 39% to 44% of your income. If the insurance money pays the balance down, or a longer amortization lowers the payment, you may fit easily.
  • Use an alternative lender (a B-lender). If your income is tight or your credit took a hit during a hard time, B-lenders can sometimes go up to roughly 50% debt ratios and can work with credit scores below 600, depending on the whole file. Rates and fees are higher, so it's often a plan for one term while things settle.
  • Use a private lender for a short time. If the estate takes months to settle, a private mortgage can bridge the gap. Private lenders focus on the home's equity and usually want you to keep at least 20% to 25% equity or more. This is a short-term tool with higher costs, not a long-term home.
  • Look at a reverse mortgage. If you're 55 or older, a reverse mortgage can pay off the existing mortgage so there are no monthly payments. You keep ownership and the balance is repaid when the home is sold. Our post on getting a mortgage when you're retired covers how pension income is counted too.
  • Sell and move on your own terms. Sometimes the house is simply more than you want to carry. Selling isn't failure. It can free up equity and give you breathing room. If you decide to sell, try not to rush the timing.

If you need to restructure the mortgage, our refinancing page explains how a refinance works and what it costs.

A Simple Checklist for the First Few Months

  1. Keep the payments going if you can. A missed payment makes everything harder.
  2. Call the lender. Ask what they need, whether creditor insurance was in place, and what help they offer.
  3. Find the papers. Your mortgage statement, the title, any life insurance policies and the will.
  4. Get several copies of the death certificate. The lender, land titles, insurers and banks will all ask for one.
  5. Talk to a lawyer about the title and the estate.
  6. Apply for CPP benefits and any workplace survivor benefits.
  7. Mark your renewal date. Start looking at options about four months before it.
  8. Hold off on big decisions for a while if you can. You don't have to sell or refinance in the first few weeks.

Frequently Asked Questions

What happens to my mortgage if my spouse dies?

The mortgage stays in place. If you were both on it, you're responsible for the full balance and the terms carry on as long as payments continue. If only your spouse was on it, the debt belongs to their estate, and you may need to apply to put it in your name to keep the home.

Does the mortgage have to be paid off when someone dies?

Usually not right away. Most lenders don't call the mortgage due just because a borrower died, as long as the payments keep coming. The mortgage is paid off only if creditor insurance or a life insurance payout covers it, or if the home is sold.

Do I have to requalify for the mortgage after my spouse dies?

Not usually, if you were both on the mortgage. Your income is looked at again when you refinance, take the mortgage over in your own name, or at renewal if your lender treats removing your spouse as a new application. A straight switch to a new lender at renewal no longer needs the stress test at most federally regulated lenders.

Does mortgage insurance pay off the mortgage when you die?

It depends on the type. Mortgage default insurance, paid when you put less than 20% down, protects the lender and does not pay off the mortgage. Creditor insurance from the lender pays the balance if the person was covered and the claim is approved. Personal life insurance pays your beneficiary, who can use it for the mortgage.

How do I remove my deceased spouse's name from the title in Alberta?

If you held title as joint tenants, you send the Alberta land titles office proof of death, such as a death certificate, with the required form, and the home is transferred into your name. Probate isn't needed for this step. If title was held as tenants in common, the share goes through the estate, so talk to a lawyer.
