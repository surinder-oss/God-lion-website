What Happens to a Variable Rate Mortgage After a Rate Hike, and Should You Lock In?

Your neighbour says their mortgage payment went up this month. You check your own statement and the payment is exactly the same. So who's right? You both are. You just have two different kinds of variable mortgage.

If you have a variable rate mortgage after a rate hike, the first question isn't "what will rates do next?" Nobody can tell you that for sure. The better question is "what kind of variable mortgage do I have, and what is the hike doing to it?" This guide walks you through it in plain words: why some payments changed and others didn't, what a trigger point is, how locking in usually works, and what to check on your statement this month.

Why Some Variable Payments Went Up and Others Didn't

When the Bank of Canada raises its rate, the big lenders raise their prime rate soon after. Your variable rate is set as prime plus or minus a number, so your rate goes up too. As of November 2026, the most recent example was the Bank of Canada's increase on October 28, 2026. What happens next depends on how your payment is set up.

There are two main types:

  • Adjustable payment variable. Your payment changes when the rate changes. Rate goes up, payment goes up. The amount going to principal stays about the same, so your payoff date doesn't move.
  • Fixed payment variable. Your payment stays the same. When the rate goes up, more of each payment goes to interest and less goes to paying down what you owe. Your balance shrinks more slowly.

So if your payment didn't change, that doesn't mean the hike missed you. It means the cost is showing up in your balance instead of your bank account. That's easy to miss, and it's the one that can cause trouble later.

Most big banks offer the fixed payment type. Many monoline and broker-channel lenders offer the adjustable type. Some lenders offer both. Your mortgage contract or your lender's online portal will tell you which one you have.

A Simple Example: Where Your Payment Actually Goes

Here's how a variable rate mortgage after a rate hike looks with round numbers. These are made up to show the idea, not real rates.

Say your monthly payment is $2,500. Before the hike, $1,400 went to interest and $1,100 went to principal.

  • Adjustable payment: your interest goes up to about $1,500. Your payment rises to about $2,600, and about $1,100 still goes to principal.
  • Fixed payment: your interest goes up to about $1,500, but your payment stays at $2,500. Now only $1,000 goes to principal. That's $100 a month less coming off your balance.

One hike doesn't change much. But if rates keep rising, the principal part of a fixed payment keeps shrinking. That leads to the next question people ask.

What Is a Trigger Point on a Variable Rate Mortgage?

On a fixed payment variable mortgage, there's a rate where your payment only covers the interest. Above that rate, nothing goes to principal. People call this the trigger rate.

Most contracts also have a second line, often called the trigger point. That's when the unpaid interest has built up so much that your balance passes a limit in your contract. When you hit it, the lender will contact you. The Financial Consumer Agency of Canada notes that the trigger point is listed in your mortgage contract, and that your lender may raise your payment when you reach it.

If you reach your trigger point, you'll usually get a short window to pick one of these:

  1. Make a lump-sum payment to bring your balance back on track.
  2. Raise your regular payment so it covers interest and principal again.
  3. Convert to a fixed rate, if your mortgage allows it.

The details are different at every lender. Some don't charge a prepayment fee for these steps. Some do if the change pays your mortgage off faster than your original schedule. Read the letter closely, and call us before you choose.

The good news: you rarely go from fine to trigger point overnight. If you check your statement now, you'll see it coming months ahead.

Should You Lock In Your Variable Rate Mortgage After a Rate Hike?

Most variable mortgages let you convert to a fixed rate partway through your term, with your current lender, at their fixed rate on the day you convert. Many lenders don't charge a penalty for it, but terms and fees vary. Our full guide on converting a variable mortgage to fixed covers how it works step by step.

Locking in isn't always the right move. Fixed rates are driven by the bond market, not the Bank of Canada, so they don't always move together. When you lock in, you also give up the variable's usually smaller penalty if you need to break the mortgage early. Our guide to how mortgage penalties are calculated shows how big that difference can be.

Locking in often makes sense when:

  • You have a fixed payment variable and you're getting close to your trigger rate.
  • A higher payment would put real strain on your budget, and you need it to stay the same.
  • You're losing sleep over it. Peace of mind counts.

Staying variable often makes sense when you have room in your budget, you might sell or refinance in the next few years, or your renewal is coming up soon anyway. For the bigger picture, see our variable vs fixed rate mortgage guide.

What to Check on Your Mortgage Statement This Month

The best thing you can do with a variable rate mortgage after a rate hike is look at the numbers. Pull up your latest statement or log in to your lender's portal. Look for these five things:

  1. Your payment type. Adjustable or fixed payment? If your payment amount changed after the hike, it's adjustable.
  2. Your current rate. It should now show the higher rate.
  3. Interest vs principal. How much of your last payment went to each? Compare it to a statement from a few months ago.
  4. Your remaining amortization. If it's longer than it was at the start of your term, your balance is paying down slower than planned.
  5. Your renewal date. If it's within the next four to six months, your options open up.

If you can't find any of this, call your lender and ask. You can also send the statement to us and we'll go through it with you.

If Your Renewal Is Coming Up Soon

If your term ends in the next few months, you may not need to convert at all. You can pick a new rate type at renewal.

Many lenders will hold a rate for you up to about 120 days before your renewal. It's usually free. If rates rise before your renewal, you keep the held rate. If they drop, a good broker will check for a lower one before you sign.

You don't have to stay with your current lender either. Since November 2024, if you switch lenders at renewal without adding money or changing the amortization, you usually don't have to pass the stress test again. Our post on switching lenders at renewal without the stress test explains who qualifies.

If the hike has already made money tight, tell us early. Depending on your file, options can include a longer amortization with your current lender, a switch to a lender with different rules, or, if your credit has taken a hit, an alternative lender as a short-term bridge. Lenders are also expected to work with borrowers who are struggling, so it's worth asking yours what help they offer before you miss a payment.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does a rate hike affect my variable rate mortgage right away?

Usually within days. Lenders change their prime rate soon after the Bank of Canada moves, and your variable rate follows prime. With an adjustable payment, your payment goes up. With a fixed payment, your payment stays the same but more of it goes to interest.

Why didn't my variable mortgage payment go up?

You likely have a fixed payment variable mortgage. Your payment stays the same, but more of it now goes to interest and less to principal. Your balance goes down more slowly, so check your statement to see the new split.

What happens when you hit your trigger rate?

At the trigger rate, your payment only covers interest. If unpaid interest keeps adding up and your balance reaches the trigger point in your contract, your lender will ask you to act. That usually means a lump-sum payment, a higher regular payment, or converting to a fixed rate.

How do I know if my variable mortgage has a fixed or adjustable payment?

Check whether your payment amount changed after the last rate change. If it did, you have an adjustable payment. If it stayed the same, you have a fixed payment. Your mortgage contract and your lender's online portal will also say which type you have.

Should I lock in my variable rate after a rate hike?

It depends on your budget, how close you are to your trigger rate, and when you renew. Locking in gives you a steady payment but usually a higher rate and a bigger penalty if you break the mortgage. If your renewal is only a few months away, a rate hold may be the better option.
