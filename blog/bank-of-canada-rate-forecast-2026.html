Bank of Canada Rate Forecast 2026: What It Means for Your Mortgage

Book a Free Consultation

Pick a time that works for you — no obligation

Here's where things stand as of October 5, 2026. The Bank of Canada's policy rate is 2.25%. The Bank held it there again on September 2. That was the seventh decision in a row with no change. Two decisions are left this year: October 28 and December 9.

What changed in September was the tone. The Bank said the risk of higher inflation has grown, and it said it is ready to adjust policy if needed. Earlier in the year, most forecasters expected a flat line for 2026. Now some are asking whether a hike could come sooner. Nobody knows for sure, and we don't try to guess. What you can do is set up your mortgage so it works whether the Bank holds, hikes or cuts.

Where Things Sit Right Now

The baseline, as of October 5, 2026:

  • Policy rate: 2.25%, unchanged at seven straight decisions (most recently September 2, 2026)
  • Bank Rate: 2.50%. Deposit rate: 2.20%
  • Prime rate: lenders' prime rates move with the policy rate, so prime has not moved since the Bank's last cut on October 29, 2025
  • Next decision: Wednesday, October 28, 2026 at 9:45 a.m. ET, with a full Monetary Policy Report
  • Last decision of the year: Wednesday, December 9, 2026
  • Stress test: uninsured borrowers still qualify at the higher of 5.25% or their contract rate plus 2 percentage points

What the Bank Said in September

The September 2 decision kept the rate at 2.25%, but the message leaned a little more toward caution on inflation. The main points:

  • Growth picked up. GDP grew 3.3% in the second quarter after a weak start to the year.
  • Jobs held up. Unemployment edged down to 6.4% in July.
  • Inflation is running warm. Headline CPI was around 3%, pushed up mostly by gasoline prices.
  • Trade is still a wild card. New tariffs between Canada and the U.S. add uncertainty.
  • The key line: the Bank said upside risks to inflation have increased and it is prepared to adjust policy as needed.

Put simply, the Bank has reasons not to cut and it's watching for reasons to hike. That's why the October 28 decision, with its full forecast update, is the one to watch. Our piece on oil and Canadian mortgage rates explains how energy prices feed into all of this.

How the Outlook Shifted Through 2026

In the spring, forecasters were split. Some saw hikes later in 2026, others saw no change. Through the summer, as the Bank kept holding, most of them settled on "no change this year." After September, the conversation moved again. Higher inflation risk has some people asking about a hike at one of the last two meetings.

The lesson from this year: forecasts change every few months. A mortgage plan that only works if one forecast comes true is a weak plan.

Why Fixed Rates Don't Wait for the Bank

Here's the part many people miss. The Bank of Canada sets the overnight rate. That moves prime, and prime moves variable mortgages. Fixed mortgage rates mostly follow Government of Canada bond yields, not the policy rate.

So the Bank can hold for seven meetings in a row while fixed rates still move up or down underneath you. When bond markets expect higher inflation, fixed pricing can rise before the Bank does anything. That's why a rate hold on a pre-approval matters more than the decision calendar.

If You Are Renewing

This is where most of the money is, and it has little to do with the forecast.

  • Don't sign the first offer. Your renewal letter is a starting point. Comparing it against the wider market often finds a better deal.
  • Switching at renewal is stress-test free. Since November 2024 you can move an insured or uninsured mortgage to a new lender at renewal without re-qualifying under the stress test, as long as you don't add to the balance or stretch the amortization. Our guide to switching lenders covers how it works, and the 2026 renewal strategy guide runs the switch-versus-stay math.
  • Read the letter properly. Most people skim it and miss the deadline and what happens if they do nothing. Here's what to do when the renewal letter lands.
  • Look at the term, not just the rate. If you don't want to lock in for five years while the outlook is unclear, a shorter term may cost very little more. We can price both.
  • Start four to six months out. Many lenders let you hold a renewal rate ahead of time. Starting early gives you room to compare.

If You Are on a Variable

A variable rate moves with prime, and prime moves with the Bank. If the Bank holds in October and December, your rate doesn't change because of the Bank.

  • Size the downside now. Ask us to show you what a 0.25 or 0.50 point increase would do to your payment. If that number would break your budget, that tells you more than any forecast.
  • Know your payment type. Some variable mortgages have a payment that changes with prime. Others keep the payment the same and change how much goes to interest. With the second type, rate increases can stretch out your payoff.
  • Not every lender uses the same prime. Some price variable mortgages off their own prime. Ask what prime your rate is built on before comparing discounts.
  • Converting isn't free. Locking a variable into a fixed mid-term is usually priced off a conversion rate, not the lender's regular new-client rate. See our guide to converting a variable to fixed.

Our variable versus fixed guide works through the math on a sample file.

If You Are Shopping or Pre-Approved

  • Get a rate hold. Many lenders hold a rate for up to 90 to 120 days on a pre-approval. If fixed rates rise, you're protected. If they fall, many lenders will give you the lower rate.
  • Know what the hold covers. A pre-approval holds a rate, but the final approval still depends on the property and your file. Our pre-approval guide explains what a strong one looks like.
  • Budget past the down payment. Legal fees, title insurance and adjustments add up. Here's what closing costs in Alberta usually run.
  • Don't try to time the market. Waiting for the perfect week rarely pays off. Buying on a solid pre-approval usually beats chasing the bottom.

The Part Nobody Forecasts: Your Own File

Most mortgage plans don't fall apart because the Bank moved a quarter point. They fall apart because the file changed and nobody planned for it. Income shifted. Someone became self-employed. Credit took a hit. A separation happened. Those things affect your options far more than the policy rate, and you can see them coming.

It also helps to know the full range of lenders:

  • A-lenders (banks, credit unions and monoline lenders) usually cost the least when the file is clean and the income documents line up.
  • B-lenders are the middle ground. Depending on the file, they can work with credit below the usual A-lender cut-off, allow higher debt ratios, and for some self-employed borrowers use about 12 months of business bank statements instead of the latest Notice of Assessment. See our B-lender guide.
  • Private lenders focus on equity and are the most flexible on income. They cost more and usually need 20% to 25% or more down. See our private lender guide.

Anyone who says there's only one path is describing one lender, not the market. If you're self-employed, the gap between these channels can matter far more than any rate decision.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the Bank of Canada's interest rate right now?

As of October 5, 2026, the policy rate is 2.25%. The Bank held it there on September 2, 2026, the seventh decision in a row with no change. The Bank Rate is 2.50% and the deposit rate is 2.20%.

When is the next Bank of Canada rate announcement?

The next announcement is Wednesday, October 28, 2026 at 9:45 a.m. ET. It comes with a full Monetary Policy Report. The last decision of 2026 is Wednesday, December 9.

Will the Bank of Canada raise rates in 2026?

Nobody knows for sure. In September the Bank said upside risks to inflation have increased and it is prepared to adjust policy as needed. That has some forecasters talking about a possible hike, while others still expect a hold. Forecasts are not promises. A mortgage plan should hold up either way.

What does a Bank of Canada hold mean for my mortgage?

If the Bank holds, prime stays put, so variable rates don't change because of the Bank. Fixed rates are different. They mostly follow Government of Canada bond yields, so they can still move up or down while the Bank stands still.

Should I go variable or fixed right now?

It depends on your file, not the forecast. Variable can suit a household with steady income and room to absorb a payment change. Fixed suits someone who needs a set payment to budget around. A shorter fixed term is another option if you don't want to commit for five years. The honest answer comes from running all three on your real numbers.

Should I wait to renew until the Bank of Canada decides?

Usually not. Waiting only helps if rates fall. The bigger savings at renewal come from what you control: comparing the offer instead of signing the letter, switching lenders if it pays, and picking the right term. Since November 2024 you can switch lenders at renewal without re-qualifying under the stress test, as long as you don't add to the balance or the amortization.
