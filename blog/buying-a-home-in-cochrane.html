Buying a Home in Cochrane, Alberta: What Commuters and Acreage Buyers Should Know

Book a Free Consultation

Pick a time that works for you — no obligation

You drive west out of Calgary on a Saturday, the foothills open up, and by the time you crest the hill above town you're scrolling listings. That's how a lot of people end up buying a home in Cochrane. The views sell it. The mortgage is where the details live.

Cochrane isn't like Airdrie or Okotoks. It sits right in the Bow Valley corridor, so you get a mix of brand-new subdivisions, older homes in town, and acreages on wells and septic just past the town line. Each one finances a little differently. This guide covers what it costs, what lenders look at, and how to plan for the commute before you write an offer.

What It Costs When Buying a Home in Cochrane

As of October 2026, the Calgary Real Estate Board (CREB) put the Cochrane benchmark price at about $571,000. That's a little lower than a year earlier, and more homes have been sitting on the market than in the last few years. For buyers, that usually means more choice and a bit more room to negotiate. You can check the latest monthly numbers on CREB's housing statistics page.

Here's what the minimum down payment looks like on a home near that benchmark, if you qualify for an insured mortgage:

  • 5% of the first $500,000 = $25,000
  • 10% of the remaining $71,000 = $7,100
  • Total minimum down payment: about $32,100

If you put down less than 20%, you'll pay a mortgage default insurance premium, which gets added to your mortgage. Put down 20% or more (about $114,200 on a $571,000 home) and you skip the premium. Alberta has no land transfer tax, but you still need cash for legal fees, title insurance, a home inspection and moving. A good rule is to have at least 1.5% of the price set aside for closing costs.

Prices vary a lot by type. Townhomes and condos in town sit well below the benchmark. Newer detached homes and anything with land can sit well above it. So the real question is not "what does Cochrane cost" but "what does the home you want cost, and what does that do to your payment."

Buying a Home in Cochrane as a Commuter: Budget for the Drive

Most people buying a home in Cochrane still work in Calgary at least a few days a week. The rebuilt Highway 1A and 22 interchange has helped, but you're still looking at a real daily drive, and that costs money.

Here's the thing most buyers miss. Lenders don't count your commute when they work out how much you can borrow. They look at your income, your debts, property tax, heat and any condo fees. Gas, a second car, and winter tires don't show up in their math at all.

So you can be approved for a payment that feels tight once you add $300 to $600 a month in driving costs. Before you shop, we like to work out two numbers with you:

  1. What the lender says you can borrow.
  2. What you're comfortable paying once the commute is in the budget.

Buy based on the second number. If you're planning to buy a second vehicle for the commute, do it after you close, not before. A new car loan right before closing can shrink your approval or put a deal at risk.

Buying an Acreage or a Home on Well and Septic Near Cochrane

A lot of what draws people west is space. Homes on a few acres in Rocky View County, just outside the town, are a big part of the Cochrane market. These homes can be great, but lenders treat them differently from a house on a town lot.

What lenders usually want to see on a rural property near Cochrane:

  • Water tests. Most lenders want a potability test on the well, and many want a flow test too. Make well testing a condition of your offer.
  • A septic check. Some lenders ask for a septic inspection. Even when they don't, you should get one.
  • A full appraisal. Rural homes have fewer sales to compare against, so appraisals can take longer and come in more carefully.
  • Year-round road access. The home has to be reachable in winter.

The biggest surprise for most buyers is that the lender may not lend on all the land. Many lenders base their value on the house plus a limited amount of land, often around the first few acres. Shops, barns and extra acreage may count for little or nothing. On larger or more farm-like properties, many lenders want a conventional mortgage with 20% or more down instead of an insured one. We go deeper on all of this in our acreage mortgage guide.

There's also a tax and services difference. A home inside the Town of Cochrane and a home a few minutes out in Rocky View County can have different property taxes and different services, such as water, sewer and snow clearing. Those costs flow into your qualifying numbers, so look them up for the exact property.

How a Mortgage Broker in Cochrane Helps When Your File Is Different

The Cochrane market pulls in a wide mix of buyers. Young families stretching for their first detached home. Self-employed trades people who work all over the Bow Valley. Couples who sold in Calgary and want land. People priced out of Canmore who still want the mountains close.

A single bank has one set of rules. When your file fits those rules, great. When it doesn't, you need more options. Here's how the range usually looks:

  • A-lenders (banks, credit unions and monoline lenders) usually offer the most competitive pricing for clean files. They usually work within debt ratios of about 39% of income for housing costs and 44% for all debts. Self-employed buyers are often qualified on a two-year average of their declared income, or through a stated-income program.
  • B-lenders can work with bruised credit, sometimes below a 600 credit score depending on the overall file. They can go to debt ratios of roughly 50/50. For self-employed buyers, many can use about 12 months of business bank statements instead of the latest Notice of Assessment.
  • Private lenders focus mostly on the property and your equity. Expect to need 20% to 25% or more down. Private money is usually a short-term bridge back to a regular lender, and some private lenders will look at rural homes that others pass on.

Which one fits depends on your income, credit, down payment and the property itself. That last part matters more in Cochrane than in most places. A great file on an unusual property can still get turned down by the wrong lender.

Renewing or Refinancing in Cochrane

Cochrane grew fast in the last five years. A lot of owners bought around 2021 on low five-year fixed rates, and many of those terms are coming up for renewal now.

If that's you, don't just sign the renewal letter your lender mails out. Since late 2024, if you switch to a new lender at renewal without adding to your mortgage or stretching the amortization, you generally don't have to pass the stress test again. That gives you real room to shop. Start looking about four months before your renewal date.

If you want to pull out equity for a basement suite, a shop on your acreage or to pay off higher-interest debt, a refinance is a different process. It means a new appraisal and a full qualification. On rural homes, the same land and well rules from above come back into play. Our mortgage renewal page has more on how to plan it.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much do I need to buy a house in Cochrane?

As of October 2026, the Cochrane benchmark price was about $571,000. On an insured mortgage, the minimum down payment on that price is about $32,100. You'll also want at least 1.5% of the price for closing costs like legal fees and an inspection.

Is it harder to get a mortgage on an acreage near Cochrane?

It can be. Lenders want water and septic checks, a full appraisal and year-round access. Many only lend on the house and a limited amount of land, and larger properties often need 20% or more down. A broker can match the property to lenders who are comfortable with it.

Do lenders count commuting costs when I buy in Cochrane?

No. Lenders look at your income, debts, property tax, heat and condo fees. Gas and vehicle costs aren't part of their math, so build them into your own budget before you decide how much to spend.

Can I get a mortgage in Cochrane if I'm self-employed?

Yes, in many cases. A-lenders often use a two-year average of your declared income or a stated-income program. Some B-lenders can use about 12 months of business bank statements instead. The right path depends on your file.

Should I switch lenders when my Cochrane mortgage renews?

It's worth comparing. If you switch at renewal without adding to your mortgage or extending the amortization, you generally won't need to pass the stress test again. Start shopping about four months before your renewal date.
